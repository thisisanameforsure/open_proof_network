A public record of what has been proved, and of how far anyone understands it.

If you have read the declaration of 2026-09-11, you know what goes wrong when a proof arrives from a machine and nobody stands behind it. The record of the last year is not a record of wrong proofs. It is a record of problems announced as solved that were already in the literature; of formal statements that did not say what the conjecture said, so that a true theorem was proved about the wrong object; of results announced by press release with a citation missing; and of kernel-checked derivations that added, in the words of the people whose problems they were, almost nothing to what anyone understood. The declaration's own phrase for the activity is the mass production of true-or-false statements, and its central fear is that the people who would have taken an idea and made it live are cut out of the chain.

This network is built for that objection, not around it. An open problem is listed here only after a curator has searched the literature for it and recorded what was found. It becomes claimable only while a named mathematician, its steward, has signed a commitment to understand and write up whatever the network produces on it. Whoever signs that a formal statement means what the conjecture means takes no proof credit on the problem, so nobody profits from a statement slightly weaker than the one that was asked. Every attempt, failed or not, is a published, typed record. When a proof merges the problem is marked resolved and, in the same breath, undigested, until a person who can explain the proof without the tool that produced it has signed the explainer and until a paper or note exists; the source registry hears "kernel-checked, not yet explained", not "solved". The network announces nothing. It keeps no leaderboard. It publishes its failures.

What the machines bring, when people stay in the chain, is real. A resolution of an Erdős problem in 2026 became an eight-author paper that generalised the method; a disproof in discrete geometry got a companion paper by nine people and was then improved by hand; a formal proof of Sendov's conjecture was digested by a mathematician from about ninety thousand lines to about fifteen thousand, and found to be, in his word, elementary. In each case the value was created after the certificate, by a person. The record below is organised so that the person is named, the certificate is a fact, and the difference between the two is never hidden.
