Propose a problem

A mathematician may put an open problem forward in ordinary mathematics, with references, on the graph repository: the proposal form. No Lean is needed; formalizing the statement is open work that anyone may take, and you check the result against your conjecture afterwards. Intake stays curated, so a curator reads the prior art you cite before anything is listed. Unless you decline, you become the problem's steward (D-6 v3.17). A problem you want kept private is declined, never held back.

How a statement is reviewed

A conjecture proposed in words becomes Lean through a formalization review, not one curator's statement (D-9 v3.39): people and models each submit a candidate blind, the gate compiles, screens and runs intent tests against every one and keeps a discarded candidate on the record with the kernel-checked exhibit that discarded it, and candidates proved equivalent in both directions share a cluster. What the candidates disagree on, and every mutant the tests miss, becomes a question in mathematics to the intent signer (the proposer, or the problem's steward), whose answer becomes a test. Two named roles sign on the review's own page, the intent signer and an independent signer who reads the Lean rendered as written; a proof that settles the root merges only once the review is complete, unless catalog evidence already vouches for the statement.

If you proposed the problem. You are asked questions, never Lean: each one is written as the hypothesis it tests (“suppose 5 were the only prime — would the conjecture hold at n = 2?”), beside the small case that raised it, and you answer on the problem’s review page signed in with GitHub, citing a source where you have one. The GitHub App mentions you on the review’s tracking issue when questions wait, so the network never holds your email. A problem that existed before reviews keeps its grade and stays open to work while its review runs in the background; nothing leaves the frontier to wait for an answer. Agents and people who write Lean will find the candidate format, the blind rule and the open pairs in the contributor guide’s “Formalization review” section.

Registries

Palomar is a registry of Lean-verified results run by the Lean FRO and ICARM. It indexes a public repository at one commit: a statement, a proof and a licence. It checks a proof with two independent kernels and reviews the statement with a model; that is, in its own words, "not peer review". A registered entry is a checked claim with named authors, not a judgment of its worth.

What the network registers. A resolved open-track problem, or a formalization-track one a curator judges of research interest (D-10 v3.37). The entry states the fidelity grade as the record carries it; nothing is announced beyond that. Who registers. A curator, after reading the registry's review; never an agent on its own. The gate exports the statement and the proof's closure as a repository in the registry's shape, the curator submits it, and the registration is appended to the problem as a record, so the problem's page says whether it is submitted, registered, asked for revisions or declined. How a contributor appears. As an author, by pseudonym, or by a display name they record through the service; the authors are the ledger's identities and never a model (D-19 v3.37). Recording a name is a one-way publication: a later record withdraws it from the network's side, and the registry keeps what it has already indexed. What the network reads back. The registry's public feed is read for prior art at intake and for literature proposals. A literature record may cite a Palomar entry by its id, PALOMAR-YYYY-MM-DD-NNNNNN vN, and a steward or curator confirms it or not. A repository the network exports carries Apache-2.0 alone (D-23 v3.37).

The Leiden Declaration, recommendation by recommendation

The Leiden Declaration on AI and Mathematics (2026-06-02) and the declaration of 2026-09-11 by twenty-five Fields medallists say what a proof network owes the community. The network is not an entity and endorses nothing formally; the table says, for each objection the community has made, what the protocol does about it and what it does not, so a reader can check the claim against the record rather than take it on trust. How to read a proof page

Every proof on the network is written in Lean, a language in which a proof is a program and a small checker, the kernel, accepts it or does not. You do not need to read Lean to follow one here. Each statement has a page, and each way a problem has been proved has a reading view (on the problem's page, under its proofs) that lays the proof out as you would read a paper: the problem in words first, with its formal statement and whether that statement has been audited, then every statement the proof rests on, lemmas before the results that use them. A table at the end ties each statement to its Lean declaration, the file and lines that prove it, and the gate's record of the check.

Finding your way. A problem that has been proved says how, in words, under How it was proved (in words, unverified): the opening of its proof's explainer, with who wrote it, and the sources the record cites. The reading view opens with a table of contents, and reads dependencies first; a button turns it round to read root first, the problem's own proof before what it rests on. A statement's Lean is always written := by sorry: the sorry holds the place of a proof, because a statement file only says what is claimed, and a proof the gate checked is its own file, Proof.lean. A statement page lists what the statement depends on and marks each dependency the proof's term uses, or one it declared and does not use. Nodes named spec- and a hash carry their theorem's name beside the id. An outline step whose id had to escape a character (h_x3a9div) shows its Lean name beside it (hΩdiv), and a section of an explainer that covers more than six steps folds its list of steps. On a phone the words come before the steps, the statement graph scrolls sideways at full size, and wherever a desktop says hover, tap. Words waiting in an open pull request are not shown as words, but a statement page says they are coming: In review: #N by X, beneath the words they would change, read from the service when the page opens.

LabelWhat it means

The outline. Above a proof's Lean is its outline: the proof's steps in order, each with the claim it establishes and what is left to prove after it, one click from the lines of Lean that prove it. The outline is computed by the gate from the checked proof, so it says nothing the kernel did not check. Claims are printed in Lean's notation with every number's type written out, which is why a step may read a + (1 : Nat) ≤ b where a paper would write a + 1 ≤ b: the explicit form is the one that means exactly what the kernel checked. A step closed by a standard automatic tactic is labelled routine, with the tactic's name, and folded away, so the page shows where the work is; the label names the tactic and says nothing about difficulty. A step whose printed claim did not read back as the same Lean is shown as its Lean lines only, rather than as text that might mean something else. In a partial proof, a step marked hole is a gap the proof left open, and links to the statement it became. A step whose id is close is the proof's closing tactics, the ones after its named steps that finish the goal; a case step lists the hypotheses its split introduced.

The words. Beside each piece of Lean are words saying what it says (a gloss), and beside each proof an account of how it works (an explainer). Contributors write them, as they write proofs, and improve them version by version. A gloss opens with the statement in standard mathematical notation, written in LaTeX and mirroring the Lean exactly, every hypothesis and quantifier; then, in a few lines, any Lean convention that changes its meaning, such as floor division or truncated subtraction; prose, if any, comes last. Words a contributor's model helped draft say so (drafted with the model); a few early ones, drafted by the network itself before contributors took the work over, say machine-drafted by their model. Nothing is edited in place: each version stays in the history, with a diff to the one before, and a withdrawn version stays listed as withdrawn.

Sections, and who stands behind them. The words are read in sections. A gloss is one section; each section of an explainer covers some steps of the outline, and so some lines of Lean. Each section says in words where it stands: drafted with a model, written by a person, or read against the Lean by a steward of the problem or a curator who has signed it. A model may change only drafted words; once a person has written or verified a section, only a person may change it. A person's change to someone else's words, or to verified words, is not shown as the section's words. It appears beneath them, marked awaiting review, with a diff showing what it changes, until a steward or curator signs it; signing is how a steward approves an edit, and it is also what earns the words' author credit.

A signature claims less than it might. On a gloss it says read against the Lean: the signer has checked that the words say what the Lean says. On an explainer it says the signer can explain the proof without the tool that produced it. Neither says the mathematics is right: the kernel says that of the Lean, and nothing says it of prose. A gloss describes one exact version of its file; if the file has since changed, the gloss is shown only in the history. The site takes no input, so the way to write or improve any of these words is the contributor guide's section on glosses, explainers and outlines, through the service, an agent, or a pull request.
