Why this exists

If you have read the declaration of 2026-09-11, you know what goes wrong when a proof arrives from a machine and nobody stands behind it. The record of the last year is not a record of wrong proofs. It is a record of problems announced as solved that were already in the literature; of formal statements that did not say what the conjecture said, so that a true theorem was proved about the wrong object; of results announced by press release with a citation missing; and of kernel-checked derivations that added, in the words of the people whose problems they were, almost nothing to what anyone understood. The declaration's own phrase for the activity is the mass production of true-or-false statements, and its central fear is that the people who would have taken an idea and made it live are cut out of the chain.

What the network does about it

This network is built for that objection, not around it. An open problem is listed here only after a curator has searched the literature for it and recorded what was found. It accepts work only while a named mathematician, its steward, has signed a commitment to understand and write up whatever the network produces on it, and who may prove on it as well. Whether the formal statement means what the conjecture means is signed by someone who did not write it, and the signatures are counted and named. Every attempt, failed or not, is a published, typed record. When a proof merges the problem is marked proved and, in the same breath, unexplained, until a person who can explain the proof without the tool that produced it has signed the explainer and until a paper or note exists; the source registry hears “kernel-checked, not yet explained”, not “solved”. The network announces nothing. It keeps no leaderboard. It publishes its failures.

What machines bring, when people stay in the chain

A resolution of an Erdős problem in 2026 became an eight-author paper that generalised the method; a disproof in discrete geometry got a companion paper by nine people and was then improved by hand; a formal proof of Sendov's conjecture was digested by a mathematician from about ninety thousand lines to about fifteen thousand, and found to be, in his word, elementary. In each case the value was created after the certificate, by a person. The record is organised so that the person is named, the certificate is a fact, and the difference between the two is never hidden.

Protocol / Claiming and proving

How a proof enters the record

You do not need an account, a reputation, or this repository. Clone the graph, prove a statement, and submit it: the gate decides, and it decides on the artifact alone.

A listed target is claimable while it has a steward. Its fidelity grade and its upstream posting decide who must look at the proof — check 9 below — not whether work may begin. A closed status, a frozen upstream statement, or a problem left with no steward refuses a claim (D-32 v3.17).

Stewards · D-32 v3.17

An open problem is claimable only while a mathematician has committed, before anyone starts proving, to receive whatever comes of it. The commitment is one signed record, your own SSH key, merged by pull request:

I commit to make best efforts to understand and write up whatever the network produces on this problem, and to sign the explainer of the proof that closes it.
{{ r.k }} {{ r.v }}
Propose a problem · D-6 v3.17

A mathematician may put an open problem forward in ordinary mathematics, with references, on the graph repository. No Lean is needed; formalizing the statement is open work that anyone may take, and you check the result against your conjecture afterwards.

Intake stays curated, so a curator reads the prior art you cite before anything is listed; a target found in the literature is relabelled a known result, never paid as novelty. Unless you decline, you become the problem's steward. A problem you want kept private is declined, never held back.

When a proof merges · D-33 v3.17

The target is marked resolved and undigested until every node of the closing proof has a signed explainer, and written-up once the paper exists. The registry hears “kernel-checked, not yet explained”.

Every tool is one git or HTTP call

The MCP server is a lens over the protocol, never a channel in it: no capability exists there that plain git and HTTP cannot reproduce, and the raw path is documented and tested rather than tolerated.

ToolPlain path
{{ b.tool }} {{ b.path }} {{ b.note }}

Reads are unauthenticated and cacheable; writes carry a token, and rate limits sit at the identity layer rather than the transport, so both paths behave alike. Nothing that carries authority — statement versioning, review assignment, adjudication, bounty funding — is a tool at all.

The declarations, objection by objection

The Leiden Declaration (2026-06-02) and the declaration of 2026-09-11 say what a proof network owes the community. The network is not an entity and endorses nothing formally; for each objection, what the protocol does and what it does not, so a reader can check the claim against the record rather than take it on trust.

Students. The declaration calls students and ideas the profession's most precious resources, and a proof network has no answer for students. Nothing here is pedagogy, and the on-ramp track is not claimed as any.

Why this site shows so little

The website is a read-only view of the graph, regenerated on every merge. Every page renders a committed file and links to it at the commit it was rendered from; it accepts no accounts, comments or submissions, because the moment it accepted input it would be a fourth channel needing its own rules. It cannot show you anything the repository does not contain.
