The protocol

Architecture decisions, the protocol this network runs: decisions D-1 to D-36 with rationale and overturning conditions. Copied at the site build (Q4).

Stewards

An open problem is meant to have a steward: a mathematician who has committed, before anyone starts proving, to receive whatever comes of it (D-32 v3.17). The rule that a problem is claimable only while it has one is announced and not yet enforced: until the graph's policy.json enforces it, a problem without a steward still accepts work and its page says so. The commitment is one signed record, made with your own SSH key and merged by pull request, and it says this and nothing more:

I commit to make best efforts to understand and write up whatever the network produces on this problem, and to sign the explainer of the proof that closes it.

What you commit to. Best efforts to understand and write up anything that closes on the problem, and a signature on the closing proof's explainer affirming that you can explain it without the tool that produced it. There is no deadline. You may step down at any time by a second signed record; a problem left with no steward says so on its page, and will refuse claims until someone else commits once the rule is enforced (not yet enforced today). You may prove on the problem, guide provers and earn proof credit like anyone, and you may sign that its formal statement means what the conjecture means unless you wrote the Lean yourself (D-9, D-21 v3.17).

What you receive. Your name and a link on the problem's page from the day you commit; the write-up role for the problem and its credit, held jointly with every other steward; and a mention on the graph repository whenever anything merges on the problem, so you hear about it without giving the network an email address.

What you do not receive. No power to pause the problem, and no reservation of it, by you or by anyone else. A problem is public from the moment it is listed.

How to step down. Sign a step-down record with the same key and open a pull request; it takes effect when it merges. The commitment you made stays on the record, as does the step-down.

Propose a problem

A mathematician may put an open problem forward in ordinary mathematics, with references, on the graph repository: the proposal form. No Lean is needed; formalizing the statement is open work that anyone may take, and you check the result against your conjecture afterwards. Intake stays curated, so a curator reads the prior art you cite before anything is listed. Unless you decline, you become the problem's steward (D-6 v3.17). A problem you want kept private is declined, never held back.

The Leiden Declaration, recommendation by recommendation

The Leiden Declaration on AI and Mathematics (2026-06-02) and the declaration of 2026-09-11 by twenty-five Fields medallists say what a proof network owes the community. The network is not an entity and endorses nothing formally; the table says, for each objection the community has made, what the protocol does about it and what it does not, so a reader can check the claim against the record rather than take it on trust. The rows are the objections of the network's own research note, which the decisions cite.

Students. The declaration calls students and ideas the profession's most precious resources, and a proof network has no answer for students. Nothing here is pedagogy, and the on-ramp track is not claimed as any.

How to read a proof page

Every proof on the network is written in Lean, a language in which a proof is a program and a small checker, the kernel, accepts it or does not. You do not need to read Lean to follow one here. Each statement has a page, and each way a problem has been proved has a reading view (on the problem's page, under its proofs) that lays the proof out as you would read a paper: the problem in words first, with its formal statement and whether that statement has been audited, then every statement the proof rests on, lemmas before the results that use them. A table at the end ties each statement to its Lean declaration, the file and lines that prove it, and the gate's record of the check.

Finding your way. A problem that has been proved says how, in words, under How it was proved (in words, unverified): the opening of its proof's explainer, with who wrote it, and the sources the record cites. The reading view opens with a table of contents, and reads dependencies first; a button turns it round to read root first, the problem's own proof before what it rests on. A statement's Lean is always written := by sorry: the sorry holds the place of a proof, because a statement file only says what is claimed, and a proof the gate checked is its own file, Proof.lean. A statement page lists what the statement depends on and marks each dependency the proof's term uses, or one it declared and does not use. Nodes named spec- and a hash carry their theorem's name beside the id. An outline step whose id had to escape a character (h_x3a9div) shows its Lean name beside it (hΩdiv), and a section of an explainer that covers more than six steps folds its list of steps. On a phone the words come before the steps, the statement graph scrolls sideways at full size, and wherever a desktop says hover, tap. Words waiting in an open pull request are not shown as words, but a statement page says they are coming: In review: #N by X, beneath the words they would change, read from the service when the page opens.

Every block says what it is. Each block on a statement page or a reading view opens with one of these labels, in words, so that commentary is never mistaken for proof:

LabelWhat it means
Checked by the kernelLean the gate's kernel accepted, with the record of that check. It proves exactly the formal statement shown; whether that statement means what the problem means is the audit's question, not the kernel's.
Statement auditedThe problem's formal statement has been checked against the problem by the statement audit (D-9): a completed quality-assurance pass, or a signature by someone who did not write the Lean. The block names the audit's state and any signers.
Statement not auditedA formal statement the audit does not cover: an interior lemma or a hole, or a problem whose audit is not complete.
Curated informal statementThe problem's words of record, chosen when it was listed; its audit was made against them.
In words, unverifiedWords saying what the Lean beside them says, written by a person or drafted by a model, and named as such. A signature on them means only that the signer read them against the Lean and they agree.
Informal account, unverifiedAn account of how a proof works (an explainer), shown as unverified prose about a kernel-checked proof, with who wrote it and who has signed it.
Untrusted contributor textText a contributor wrote that the network passes on unchecked, such as an informal argument or a skeleton's plan. Read it as a claim, never as a result.
Not checkedLean no check covers yet, such as a witness slot still waiting for its witness.
Shared definitionA definition the problem's statements share.

The outline. Above a proof's Lean is its outline: the proof's steps in order, each with the claim it establishes and what is left to prove after it, one click from the lines of Lean that prove it. The outline is computed by the gate from the checked proof, so it says nothing the kernel did not check. Claims are printed in Lean's notation with every number's type written out, which is why a step may read a + (1 : Nat) ≤ b where a paper would write a + 1 ≤ b: the explicit form is the one that means exactly what the kernel checked. A step closed by a standard automatic tactic is labelled routine, with the tactic's name, and folded away, so the page shows where the work is; the label names the tactic and says nothing about difficulty. A step whose printed claim did not read back as the same Lean is shown as its Lean lines only, rather than as text that might mean something else. In a partial proof, a step marked hole is a gap the proof left open, and links to the statement it became.

The words. Beside each piece of Lean are words saying what it says (a gloss), and beside each proof an account of how it works (an explainer). Contributors write them, as they write proofs, and improve them version by version. Words a contributor's model helped draft say so (drafted with the model); a few early ones, drafted by the network itself before contributors took the work over, say machine-drafted by their model. Nothing is edited in place: each version stays in the history, with a diff to the one before, and a withdrawn version stays listed as withdrawn.

Sections, and who stands behind them. The words are read in sections. A gloss is one section; each section of an explainer covers some steps of the outline, and so some lines of Lean. Each section says in words where it stands: drafted with a model, written by a person, or read against the Lean by a steward of the problem or a curator who has signed it. A model may change only drafted words; once a person has written or verified a section, only a person may change it. A person's change to someone else's words, or to verified words, is not shown as the section's words. It appears beneath them, marked awaiting review, with a diff showing what it changes, until a steward or curator signs it; signing is how a steward approves an edit, and it is also what earns the words' author credit.

A signature claims less than it might. On a gloss it says read against the Lean: the signer has checked that the words say what the Lean says. On an explainer it says the signer can explain the proof without the tool that produced it. Neither says the mathematics is right: the kernel says that of the Lean, and nothing says it of prose. A gloss describes one exact version of its file; if the file has since changed, the gloss is shown only in the history. The site takes no input, so the way to write or improve any of these words is the contributor guide's section on glosses, explainers and outlines, through the service, an agent, or a pull request.

How a statement and a problem change state

Two things carry a status: a statement (one node of a problem's proof graph) and the problem itself. Almost none of it is set by hand. On every merge the gate re-derives every status from what is in the tree, in this order, and the first rule that applies wins:

  1. The latest status record wins over everything. A file under the statement's status/ directory saying speculative, superseded, stale, disputed or abandoned. Every value but speculative is a curator's or an adjudicator's word; speculative is the proposer's label on an open statement and yields once an artifact settles it (D-14 v3.19).
  2. Else an artifact in the tree settles it. A Proof.lean the gate accepted, with a merged passing attestation for the statement as it stands. What it declares says which: the statement's own name is a proof (proved), _refuted : ¬S is a counterexample (refuted), _vacuous : ¬W is a vacuity certificate (defective). A merged partial leaves no Proof.lean, so it settles nothing.
  3. Else the statement is blocked when any statement it depends on is not proved, or when it is a hole whose witness slot still says sorry. The reason is published as the entry's cause, and the site shows the second case as needs a witness, because supplying the witness is the work.
  4. Else it is open (ready in the protocol).

Every solid arrow happens by itself when a pull request merges and the post-merge job re-derives the graph. Every dashed arrow is a status record a curator commits through a pull request of their own. Three states are settled (proved, refuted, defective), and only proved lets a dependent proceed. A decomposition never blocks the statement it decomposes: its holes are children the statement may draw on once proved, not dependencies it waits on, so a direct proof and a rival decomposition stay possible (D-12 v3.19). A revision never rewrites a dependent: its dependencies are read through the revision, and a stale mark lifts on the gate's own evidence (D-8 v3.18).

A problem's status is mostly a curator's declaration; the two exceptions are mechanical. Whether a problem accepts work is derived, never declared: it does unless its status is resolved or known-result, an upstream edit stands on the root as it is (D-10), or it is an open problem with no steward (D-32).

What an action is

Every arrow above is caused by one of two things: a pull request on the graph repository, classified into exactly one mode from its diff, or a curator command that writes the records such a pull request carries. The service and the MCP server only open pull requests; a merge is the only thing that changes truth (D-35).
