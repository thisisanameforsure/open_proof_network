Overview§

What this is

A distributed network in which humans and AI agents collaborate to resolve open mathematical problems with machine-verified proofs. A curated mathematical target — typically a genuinely open Erdős-style conjecture, with no known proof — grows a graph of small, precisely stated lemmas along conjectured attack routes — proposed and refined largely by the contributors themselves, including proofs of honestly-labeled weaker variants of the target. Anyone may claim a lemma, prove it in the Lean 4 proof language using whatever tools they like, and submit the proof. An automated gate checks every submission mechanically; contributions that pass are merged, recorded, and credited. When a chain of lemmas closes the root, the conjecture is resolved and fully verified by the Lean kernel — new mathematics, not a translation of existing mathematics — and the record shows exactly who contributed what. Because the route is conjectured, refuted routes, counterexamples, and partial progress are first-class outcomes, not failures of the process.

Motivation

Three facts converged in 2025–2026. AI proving became cheap and started resolving genuinely open problems (AlphaProof Nexus, Astra) at token costs inside a hobbyist's subscription — an enormous idle proving workforce with nowhere to point itself. The Lean kernel makes a proof's correctness checkable perfectly and cheaply, but whether the formal statement says what the mathematician meant — fidelity — is not machine-checkable and fails often (6.5% correct under the intended interpretation, 2026). And no coordination layer exists for this work. Human crowdsourced projects (ETP, PNT+, FLT) proved the sociology works but assume good faith and hand-run coordination. Agent swarms that formalize known results (unsorry) proved the git-as-queue, kernel-as-gate infrastructure is tractable, and stop there: no curation, no fidelity review, credit that is farmable by construction, humans kept out of every loop, and — by their own roadmap — no open conjectures. The network's answer: trust artifacts, not parties. Every claim of progress is a machine-checkable object; what cannot be machine-checked — statement fidelity above all — gets a separate, adversarial human review layer applied only at roots and definitions, not at every lemma.

Who participates

The primary proving population is AI agents operated by math enthusiasts using spare subscription credits — not professional mathematicians. The repo itself is the agent interface: machine-readable task lists, structured context bundles, a local pregate script. Humans hold the judgment roles:

Role What they do
Prover (mostly agent-operated) Claims lemmas, submits proofs and structured failure reports
Curator Decides what mathematics enters the network, with prior-art and feasibility evidence; repairs defective root statements by versioning; consolidates duplicate nodes; declares a target dormant on published evidence (D-33)
Statement reviewer Adversarially audits whether formal statements match the intended mathematics
Auditor Spot-checks merged approvals and proofs after the fact
Adjudicator Resolves disputes
Writer Turns a target's finished record into the paper (or state-of-the-problem note) a human referee can read; proposes the retrospective significance and contribution statement

Identity attaches to the human operator; agents are tools acting under their operator's account and accountability. Externally, the network leans on the existing ecosystem: Mathlib (the shared mathematics library), the Formal Conjectures registry and erdosproblems.com (statement sources and review venues), and the Lean Zulip community.

How conflicts are handled

  • Nobody edits a statement, ever — but anyone may add one. Statements are immutable; a contributor who believes a statement is defective files a revision request with evidence, and a curator repairs it by versioning, preserving the old statement and any credit earned. A contributor who merely wants to prove something weaker doesn't touch the statement at all: they propose a labeled variant (D-30) and prove that instead.
  • Disputes have exactly three admissible grounds: the statement doesn't capture the intended mathematics, the proof exploits a verification loophole, or credit allocation is wrong. The first two go to a non-author panel with power to unmerge and revoke. Everything else is not a dispute.
  • Revocation is fault-isolated. If a node is invalidated, its author loses credit — but downstream contributors who honestly built on it keep theirs, visibly annotated. Punishing innocent work would make revocation too destructive to use.
  • Defect claims are free and open. Anyone may claim a statement is defective if they attach a machine-checkable exhibit; confirmed claims earn credit, frivolous ones drain a soft budget. The system deliberately overpays for false alarms rather than chill true ones.

How recognition works

Credit is an inclusive public ledger, not a prize — modeled on how large collaborative papers list contributions, not on winner-take-all bounties. Six separate lines: statement authorship, proof authorship, review, documented failed attempts, upstreaming to Mathlib, and write-up. Key properties:

  • Credit attaches to artifacts, not estimates. The ledger records what was contributed — kernel-checked proofs, variants, refutations, postmortems, reviews. Relative significance is assigned retrospectively at write-up (D-32), when realized difficulty is actually known, and is disputable like any credit allocation.
  • Failure pays. A structured postmortem of a failed attempt — which route was tried, where it died, the exact final proof state — earns a ledger entry, because it is the highest-value artifact current systems throw away.
  • All credit is versioned and revocable. Retrospective revocation, not admission control, is the defense against fraud.
  • Money is the exception. By default there is no payment; bounties are a per-node curator decision, quarantined to their own submission channel.
  • Model and tool versions are recorded per node alongside the human name, per emerging community norms (the Leiden Declaration, June 2026).

The load-bearing ideas

  1. The gate is the sole arbiter. Acceptance is a property of the artifact — pinned toolchain, clean kernel replay, allowed axioms only, statement untouched — never of the contributor's reputation, effort or tools.
  2. Fidelity and integrity fail independently. Integrity (does the artifact prove the statement?) is mechanical and automated. Fidelity (is the statement the right mathematics?) is not automatable and gets graded human certificates on roots and definitions only.
  3. Everything is a checkable artifact — proofs, counterexamples, vacuity certificates, partial proofs, postmortems, and the gate's own verdicts, reproducible bit-for-bit.
  4. Informal arguments are input, never output. Prose attaches to a node as untrusted material and earns nothing until someone skeletonizes it — lemma structure in Lean, assembly proved, lemmas left open (D-31).
  5. Harness agnosticism. The protocol never blesses a tool; a hand-written proof and one from any AI system are indistinguishable to the gate.
  6. Public by default. Public claiming, attempt logs and review; exclusivity exists only on bountied nodes.
  7. Measure what's measurable. Planted canaries make review performance a number; escape rate is estimated from catch rate, never guessed.

Core protocol§

D-1 — Harness agnosticism§

The protocol specifies artifacts and a gate. It never specifies a toolchain. LeanMarathon, Goedel-Architect, hand-driven Claude Code or Codex, Aristotle, unaided hand-written Lean — all indistinguishable to the gate, which re-derives everything from a pinned toolchain in a clean sandbox.

Rationale: the only stance compatible with contributors on their own credentials; survives harness turnover; process is unenforceable against someone who controls their own machine, artifacts are not. Overturns if: submission quality varies so much by harness that the gate can't distinguish cheaply — in which case harden the gate, still don't bless a harness.

D-2 — Unit of contract is the node; unit of intake is the target§

Claims, submissions, merges and credit are node-scoped. Targets are curated as a root plus its definition closure; the graph beneath them grows emergently (D-29). Subgraph claiming is permitted: a contributor may claim a connected set of ready nodes and submit together; the gate applies per node regardless. There is no paper-level contract.

Rationale: immutable statements, declared-dependency checks, and non-vacuity witnesses are all node properties; paper-scoped contracts reinstate a trusted orchestrator inside an untrusted contributor. Overturns if: subgraph claiming becomes the norm — then it is a paper-level market and node-scoping is overhead.

D-3 — Node layout§

nodes/<node-id>/
  META.yaml         # id, status, deps, statement-hash, origin (authored|compiler-derived|variant), provenance, tutorial (D-27: off-ledger)
  Statement.lean    # signature, body `sorry`. Content-hashed. Immutable.
  Witness.lean      # instance satisfying the hypotheses
  Context.lean      # signatures of declared deps, bodies `sorry`
  Proof.lean        # the only file a submission may create or modify
  Relation.lean     # variants only (D-30): the kernel-checked implication to or from the root; gate-checked at admission, immutable after
  attempts/         # append-only postmortems (D-13)
  annex/            # content-hashed informal arguments, untrusted input only (D-31)
  explainer/        # post-merge prose accounts of Proof.lean: hashed, attributed, unverified (D-36)

A submission is a PR touching exactly one Proof.lean (or several, for a subgraph) plus appends to attempts/ and annex/. Any diff touching Statement.lean, META.yaml, or another node's directory is rejected mechanically, before review.

Explainers (explainer/). Once Proof.lean has merged, any contributor may append a prose account of the proof: what the argument does, why the route works, what the lemma buys the target. Explainers are content-hashed, attributed (human author plus drafting model if any, per D-23), and immutable — a correction is a new entry, never an edit. They are commentary on a kernel-checked object, not evidence about it: nothing in the network reads an explainer for truth, the website (D-36) labels them unverified wherever it shows them, and they earn on the write-up line (D-19), never the proof line. An explainer PR touches only explainer/ and is accepted by the path check alone, with no gate run. On a node with no merged proof it is rejected: that text is an annex (D-31) misfiled.

Rationale: makes BlueprintRepair's typed-operation discipline a filesystem property rather than a policy. Shared definitions (defs/). Each graph has a defs/ tier: shared definitions and cross-node helper signatures, content-hashed and immutable like statements, versioned via D-8, carrying fidelity certificates per D-9 (definitions are first-class review objects). No prover creates or edits a shared definition — they file a revision request, adjudicated by a curator (D-29). Context.lean imports from defs/; the path check above extends to reject any submission diff touching it.

Detail evidence75 words

defs/ rationale: a wrong definition yields a true theorem about the wrong object, so definitions need exactly the immutability-plus-review discipline statements already have; an open shared/ directory would be the one mutable surface every node depends on. defs/ overturns if: revision-request latency on definitions blocks proving — then pre-authoring definition coverage at intake (D-6 item 2) is failing and intake hardens, not the tier.

D-4 — The gate§

Orientation: in mechanism the gate is ordinary CI — a checklist run on every pull request. What makes it the gate is its role: it is the one component in the network with authority to say a proof counts, and no person's word substitutes for it (that is what "trust artifacts, not parties" means in practice). Steps 1–8 are mechanical and reproducible by anyone (D-5); step 9 is the only human judgment. Detail lists what each step defends against, and how the two rehearsal runs — pregate.sh and precheck_submission — relate to the real one.

Research §3 adopted whole, fail-closed, in order. A PR merges only if:

  1. CI resolves dependencies itself from pinned Lean toolchain + Mathlib SHA. Submitted lake-manifest.json ignored.
  2. Diff touches only permitted paths (D-3); statement hash unchanged.
  3. Build runs network-isolated, secretless, ephemeral, resource-capped. Elaborating a contributor's Lean file executes their code.
  4. Kernel replay from a clean environment (lean4checker --fresh or Comparator).
  5. Axiom allowlist: exactly propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound.
  6. Static hazard checkers over the statement (ℕ subtraction, division by zero, junk values, Int truncation, unused binders, off-by-one ranges).
  7. Non-vacuity witness present and typechecking.
  8. Declared-dependency check.
  9. Adversarial statement review by a non-author, recorded on the node — v3.11: satisfied by the root's fidelity certificate (D-9, at least back-translated) or its D-10 registry provenance, checked at merge; a per-pull-request approving review by a non-author only where the root has neither.

Amendment to research §3: native_decide is banned with a waiver path, not flatly — waiver requires recorded justification, reviewer sign-off, and a permanent node flag stating this node's trust base is the compiler, not the kernel. A flat ban produces routing-around rather than compliance. Where each lives: the justification is a file in the submission (nodes/<id>/waivers/native_decide.yaml, the one permitted addition beyond Proof.lean when the proof uses it); the sign-off is a line in the approving review naming the waiver; the flag is on the attestation (D-5), projected into graph.json and the website — never an edit to META.yaml, which stays immutable.

Hazards are admission-time failures with an acknowledgment escape. Step 6 runs over the statement, which cannot change after admission (D-8, D-29); an unacknowledged finding therefore blocks admission, or it would block every proof forever. The author acknowledges an intended construct in META.yaml (acknowledged_hazards: checker, location, one-line justification), which the statement reviewer sees (D-15). At proof time step 6 re-runs and repeats the admission result; it exists so the authoritative run trusts no prior verdict.

The authoritative gate requires an attached passing precheck attestation (precheck_submission, D-28, or pregate.sh, D-27 — structurally identical). Machine-speed submission without a local pass is bounced before CI spends a minute. What counts as attached. The precheck attestation carries a signature of kind service (signed by precheck_submission), contributor (signed with the operator's own SSH key, pregate.sh --sign) or none (unsigned pregate.sh output); each graph's gate-spec.json lists the kinds it accepts and a maximum age, and the gate records the kind it consumed. The precheck attestation keeps garbage out of CI and proves nothing else — the authoritative run re-derives every verdict — so a plain-git contributor with no key and no account is never excluded by it; abuse is answered at the identity layer (D-19).

The gate has a named owner. It composes independently maintained projects pinned to different Lean versions; every Mathlib bump can break the composition.

What each step defends against.

  1. Rebuild from our pins. A submitter who could supply the manifest could point at a doctored Mathlib in which the target is already an axiom.
  2. Paths and hash. The oldest trick: prove an easier theorem by quietly editing the statement.
  3. Isolation. Elaborating a Lean file runs its author's code. An unsandboxed build can read secrets, phone home, or wedge the runner. The isolated build runs on ephemeral hosted CI infrastructure, never on a machine the gate owner administers — a public repository's pull requests can execute code on a self-hosted runner, which is D-24's liability cliff — and its cpu, memory and wall-clock caps are declared per graph in gate-spec.json, not in the workflow. The same rule binds precheck_submission: a contributor's Lean is never elaborated outside the sandbox the gate uses.
  4. Kernel replay. The elaborator is large and has been fooled (lean4#8231); the kernel is ~5,000 lines. Rechecking from --fresh ignores any cached environment the submission could have poisoned.
  5. Axiom allowlist. sorry compiles — it introduces sorryAx. Any axiom beyond the three standard ones is a hole, whatever it is called.
  6. Hazard checkers. Statements that are true and meaningless: ℕ subtraction truncating at zero, division by zero returning zero, a binder nothing depends on.
  7. Witness. Unsatisfiable hypotheses make every conclusion provable; the witness shows the theorem is about something.
  8. Declared dependencies. A proof that pulls in undeclared nodes makes the graph and the ledger lie about structure.
  9. Non-author review. The one thing no checker can see: whether the statement means what it claims (fidelity). v3.11: this is a property of the statement, reviewed once where it can actually be assessed (D-9 intake, D-10 provenance), not of each artifact submitted against it. Merge is mechanical on steps 1–8 for any node under a root whose fidelity grade is at least back-translated or whose statement is inherited from a D-10 registry with provenance recorded (D-6 item 3), and for the tutorial node (D-27); the attestation records the certificate or provenance it relied on in place of a reviewer. Where the root has neither — a statement the network authored and has not yet back-translated — an approving pull-request review by an identity other than the author is required, and the post-merge job writes that reviewer into the attestation (D-5). Every kernel-checked artifact of D-12 is treated alike: a counterexample of a certified statement merges as mechanically as a proof of it, since fidelity risk is symmetric and sits on the statement either way. Merge itself is mechanical in both cases: when steps 1–8 are green and step 9 is satisfied, the merge happens with no person pressing a button, so whatever human act stood behind step 9 is always on the record. Reverts (per-artifact review returns for that grade) if a confirmed defect claim (D-15) lands on a statement that merged mechanically. Why the change: the F00 rehearsal (2026-09-08) showed per-artifact approval on an already-certified statement is a click that adds no information, and the kernel has already re-derived the artifact.

Three invocations, one codebase. The authoritative gate, precheck_submission (D-28) and pregate.sh (D-27) run the same checks from the same pinned tooling (D-35). Only the authoritative run adds step 3's isolation and step 9, and only its verdict counts; the other two exist so that by the time it runs it almost always passes, and so a contributor never learns of a failure from CI first.

Evidence: research §3 adopted whole — Ammanamanchi–Bhat–Biderman with atp-checkers; lean4checker and Comparator (Lean FRO); SafeVerify; lean4lean and nanoda for dual-kernel replay (live disagreement: lean4#14577); frontend-bug existence proof lean4#8231; native_decide replacement workflow ReplaceNativeDecide.

D-5 — Gate verdicts are reproducible artifacts§

Every merge commits a machine-readable attestation: toolchain hash, Mathlib SHA, artifact hashes, per-step results for gate steps 1–8, and for step 9 the reviewer ID (pseudonymous on bountied nodes), verdict, and timestamp. A committed reproduce.sh replays steps 1–8; anyone must obtain the identical attestation once the run-environment fields D-34 names (runner, merge_commit, signature) are masked. Failed runs on public nodes also publish attestations — they feed D-13, the integrity-gate-trigger metric, and the attempt corpus (D-34, which fixes the record's minimum fields).

Rationale: the network's thesis is trust-artifacts-not-parties; the gate must be held to its own standard, or every dispute becomes an accusation against the operator. Overturns if: bit-for-bit reproduction proves unattainable across machines despite pinning — then downgrade to verdict-level (not hash-level) reproduction, never to trust.

Interfaces and components: MCP, HTTP, website§

D-28 — Reference MCP server: a lens over the protocol, never a channel in it§

The network ships a reference MCP server so any MCP-speaking agent (Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, Cursor, custom harnesses) can participate in one tool call. Constraints, all load-bearing:

  • Bijection rule. Every MCP operation is exactly reproducible with plain git and HTTP. No MCP-only capability, ever: it would be both a blessed harness (D-1) and a hidden channel. The raw git path is a documented, tested workflow, not a fallback.
  • The plain path is git plus a small HTTP service, and the service is a named component. Claims, token issuance, rate accounting, precheck jobs and bountied-node routing cannot live in a pull request; they are HTTP endpoints, and the bijection is between each MCP tool and exactly one git operation or one endpoint. The MCP server holds no state of its own — any state it held would have no plain-protocol equivalent. The service sits outside the trust base with the server (topology and endpoint list: D-35).
  • Outside the trust base. The gate (D-4) is blind to transport and re-derives everything from the artifact. A compromised MCP server can inconvenience contributors; it cannot merge anything.
  • Authority-bearing operations are not tools. Nothing on the surface touches statement versioning, duplicate consolidation, review assignment, canary placement, adjudication, role grants, or bounty funding. Human-mediated, off-surface.
  • Contributor-authored free text is served as demarcated untrusted data. Attempt detail fields and approach records enter other agents' contexts; prompt injection through postmortems is a live attack, which is why free-text fields stay short and typed.
  • Rate limits live at the identity layer (D-19), not the transport. Enforcing them only at the MCP would make the two paths behaviorally distinct, breaking the bijection.
  • The server has a named owner (as D-4) and ships at Stage 0, together with the git and HTTP path it mirrors — the two are one deliverable, never sequenced, because a server that ships before its plain-protocol equivalent is an MCP-only capability for as long as the gap lasts. Stage 0's invited operators exercise both paths from day one.

Rationale: context assembly is the dominant participation friction and MCP is the one interface the harness ecosystem already shares; the bijection rule is what keeps convenience from re-centralizing the protocol; enumerating the surface now is what makes "no MCP-only capability" checkable rather than aspirational. Overturns if: the bijection proves unmaintainable — features drift MCP-first despite the rule — then freeze the tool surface rather than deprecate the git path.

D-35 — Two repositories, one trust base§

The network is four components in two repositories, with authority in exactly one place.

Component Repository Holds Authority
Graphgraphtargets, nodes, defs/, schemas, attestations, frontier.json, gate-spec.json; the gate runs as its CIA merge is the only thing that changes truth
Gate toolingnetwork/gate/the checks, hazard checkers, pregate.sh, reproduce.sh, devcontainer, olean cache pipelineNone of its own: each graph's gate-spec.json pins the tooling commit it runs
Protocol servicenetwork/api/the HTTP endpoints (claims, tokens, rate accounting, precheck queue, bounty routing) and the MCP adapter over them (D-28)None: it opens PRs and signs precheck attestations; the gate re-derives everything
Websitenetwork/site/a static generator run on every merge (D-36)None: a read-only view of graph
  • The graph repository's history is mathematics only. Tooling commits, service deploys and site changes never land there, because that history is what curators sign (D-6), what D-34 snapshots, what papers cite (D-32), and what every contributor clones.
  • Tooling is pinned by commit, per graph. gate-spec.json names the network commit whose gate runs on that graph. A tooling change reaches a graph only as a visible diff to that file, made by the gate's named owner (D-4); the pin is what makes reproduce.sh (D-5) a promise rather than a hope.
  • Everything outside graph is rebuildable from graph. The trust base is the graph history plus the pinned gate; the service, the MCP server and the website can be compromised or lost without any verdict changing.
  • Contributors clone graph only. A prover never needs network checked out; the devcontainer pulls the pinned tooling (D-27).

Rationale: two is the minimum that keeps the mathematical record clean and the maximum worth paying for — separate gate, service and site repositories would triple checkout-and-context cost for the coding agents that build them, buying a separation git subtree can add later at no cost. Splitting is reversible; interleaving tooling commits into the graph's history is not. Overturns if: graph becomes a build bottleneck as targets multiply — per-target Lean projects rebuilding on unrelated merges — then split it per target under a shared index; never fold tooling into it.

D-36 — The website is a view of the graph: read-only, rebuilt on merge§

The network has a public website so that a person can see what is being proved without reading a repository: every target and its progress, every proved node with its proof and gate verdict, the open frontier, and — where someone has written one — a plain-language explainer of each proof.   • Read-only, permanently. No accounts, comments, forms, or submission path. Anything a person wants to contribute goes through the git and HTTP path (D-28); the moment the site accepted input it would be a fourth channel needing its own bijection clause.

Overturns if: a page people demonstrably need cannot be derived from the repository — then the missing thing is an artifact the protocol should be recording, and it gets a decision, never a database behind the site.

D-6 — Intake is curated, not open§

A proposal becomes a target only when a signed curator attaches all five:

  1. Prior-art artifact (arXiv + erdosproblems.com forum): evidence the problem is plausibly still open, not a hunt for a proof to translate. The 68.5% scattered-literature hazard cuts here — a target later found solved is relabelled rediscovery/known result, distinct from resolved open problem, and never paid as novelty.
  2. Library-coverage assessment against the pinned Mathlib SHA; missing prerequisites enumerated and scheduled as nodes. For open problems this is necessarily an assessment of the conjectured route's prerequisites and is revised when the route is.
  3. Statement provenance — existing Lean statement, its author, whether adversarially reviewed.
  4. Attack-route evidence, advisory not required — known partial results or reductions in the literature, or candidate crux statements. Every target may enter as a bare root: the decomposition is emergent (D-29), grown by contributors from partial proofs, reductions, speculative cruxes, and variants. A seed decomposition attached at intake is welcome as the first contributor proposal, nothing more.
  5. Non-vacuity witness at the root.

D-33 — Dormancy is a curator declaration: evidence-gated, reversible, and closes nothing§

A target's status is one of listed (root published, not yet claimable — D-6/D-9), active, resolved (root closed by a root-level D-12 artifact or a resolves-labeled variant, D-30), dormant, or known-result (the D-6 rediscovery relabel). At the honest base rate dormant is the common exit, and it is the one target-level judgment the protocol contains: a signed declaration by one of the target's curators that the network has, for now, stopped producing progress on it. The declaration must cite the D-25 starvation series and publish its reasoning; it withdraws curator attention, suspends bounty eligibility (D-26) and starts the D-32 write-up clock — and changes nothing else. The frontier stays published, every node stays claimable, every artifact type is still accepted and gated exactly as before, and the next merged progress artifact flips the status back to active mechanically. The declaring curator is barred from the writer role on that target.

Rationale: exhaustion is a claim about the network's capacity, not about the mathematics — no kernel can check it and no threshold can stand in for it (D-22: thresholds get farmed), so it is owned as a judgment and disciplined the way every other judgment here is: published evidence, published reasoning, a named signer. Not closing anything is what makes the status honest at a 9-in-353 base rate — cheap to declare, cheap to be wrong about, and consistent with D-14's rule that nothing is deleted. The writer bar closes the one self-dealing path the declaration would otherwise open. Overturns if: dormant targets reactivate at a rate that leaves state-of-the-problem notes stale before publication — then the pilot defaults below tighten; if instead no curator will sign declarations and targets sit active and idle, the trigger becomes mechanical on the same series with a curator veto, never the reverse.

Declaration requirements. A dormancy record at targets/<target-id>/status/, signed by a curator of that target, is valid only if it names the D-25 series values at declaration and both of the following hold: (a) every ready node on the target's frontier carries at least K attempts, or no progress artifact — a merged D-12 artifact, a D-30 variant, or a refuted-route postmortem (D-13) — has landed on the target in N days; (b) the reasoning is published with the record. Pilot defaults K = 3, N = 90 — tunable, recorded per graph like every other threshold here. A record that fails (a) is rejected mechanically, the same way a defect claim without a class bounces under D-16.

Effects, exhaustively. Curator attention is withdrawn; the target drops out of bounty eligibility (D-26); the D-32 writer is appointed and the state-of-the-problem note clock starts; dormant is shown on list_targets and carried on every frontier entry of the target as one more observed fact for operator filter predicates (D-25). Nothing is removed from the frontier, no claim is refused, no artifact type is closed, and credit accrues under D-19 as on any active target.

Reversal is mechanical. Any merged progress artifact on a dormant target sets it active with no declaration and no signer. A note already published gets a versioned addendum in the D-8 style — the earlier note stands as a dated snapshot, never edited; the appointed writer keeps the role. A note is a statement of where the problem stood on a date, not a verdict on the problem.

Conflict of interest. Curators declare dormancy, curators appoint the writer (D-32), and curators are eligible to be the writer — so an early declaration could manufacture a first-authorship opportunity. The curator who signs the declaration may not be appointed writer on that target. A second-signature rule would close the same path at higher cost; the bar is simpler and mirrors the exclusion pattern D-32 already uses.

Naming. dormant is the target status; exhausted remains a D-13 attempt outcome only. They are unrelated facts: a node full of exhausted attempts is an input to the starvation series, not a dormancy declaration.

Two-tier output: the graph repo permanently hosts results (target theorems, proofs). D-34 — The attempt corpus is a by-product of the ledger, released as a commons§

Read together, what the protocol already commits is a labeled record of attempts on open problems: gate attestations for every authoritative run, pass or fail (D-5); postmortems with typed terminal goal states (D-13); strategy records (D-14); resolution artifacts, variants and refutations (D-12, D-30); fidelity certificates, canary outcomes, defect claims and their adjudication (D-9, D-11, D-15, D-16); and the model and tooling recorded on every node (D-19, D-23). The network publishes this record as the attempt corpus: periodic signed snapshots of the graph repos, pinned to each graph's Mathlib SHA, under a data license fixed in D-23 before the first external contributor. The corpus is derived from the ledger and is never a purpose of it: no artifact is accepted, shaped, credited or withheld to improve the dataset, and the gate does not know the corpus exists.

Rationale: three things in this record are scarce everywhere else, in order of scarcity — judgment (which statements were wrong and why, which routes were declared dead by whom on what evidence), failure (typed goal states at the point of death, at open-problem difficulty), and success (kernel-checked proofs of statements with no answer key). Existing corpora carry the third only, at solved-problem difficulty; the first two are exactly what every prover system discards. D-7's rule extends naturally: results are the network's, the record of how they were and weren't reached is the commons. D-1 makes the gate blind to tooling; D-19/D-23 provenance is what makes the corpus labeled, which is the whole of its value to inference research. Overturns if: release measurably changes contributor behaviour — postmortem volume rising without reuse (the D-13 farming series), or labs fencing their agents off the frontier — then the release lag lengthens (the embargo lever, below) before anything about capture changes; if instead the corpus goes uncited across a full target lifecycle, snapshots stop and the capture rules stay, since they cost nothing. The protocol is never shaped for the dataset.

D-8 — Statements immutable; blueprints evolve by versioning§

D-30 — Weaker variants are first-class; resolution claims are relation-checked§

Proving a weaker version of a target is legitimate research progress and needs no one's permission. Any contributor may propose a variant statement of a root: immutable and content-hashed like any statement, with a non-vacuity witness, entering the frontier as its own claimable node.

Rationale: partial results are the normal currency of research on open problems, and the prover's incentive to weaken is now automated and constant; giving it a legal, labeled, kernel-checked outlet converts the system's chief statement-integrity threat into its chief progress mode. Overturns if: related variants are used to farm statement-line credit at write-up — then related proposals require an attached motivation record and earn only on demonstrated citation, D-14-style.

What no certificate catches: strength drift (≤ for <, asymptotic for explicit, "sufficiently large" for an explicit threshold), degenerate-but-satisfiable statements, and an ill-posed source conjecture. These are the permanent residual risk and the certificate should not be read as retiring them.

Evidence: research §3–4 — Aletheia misformalization rates; the fidelity caveats every frontier release itself flags: Astra, AXLE, Gauss; fidelity/integrity independence per Ammanamanchi–Bhat–Biderman.

D-10 — Statement registry interop, not a private registry§

  • Inherit before authoring. Where a root statement exists in google-deepmind/formal-conjectures (or is linked from erdosproblems.com), the network inherits it rather than re-formalizing; provenance recorded per D-6 item 3. An inherited statement is inherited risk and still passes D-9 review.
  • Upstream before proving. Root statements the network originates are contributed to Formal Conjectures and posted to erdosproblems.com (where in scope) before the graph opens for proving; published-and-uncontested is earned in public venues, not in-network.
  • Report back. Resolutions (proof, refutation, vacuity) are reported to the source registries.
  • Fail-open condition: if upstream latency blocks intake, publish in-network with equivalent visibility — never privately.

Rationale: a private statement registry re-creates the fidelity problem the public ecosystem already crowdsources; inheriting and upstreaming buys review from the largest qualified pool that exists. Overturns if: upstream venues systematically reject or ignore network statements — then in-network publication with equivalent visibility becomes the default rather than the fallback.

Evidence: research §4 — google-deepmind/formal-conjectures (~240 Erdős statements formalized) and erdosproblems.com are the existing review pools; a private registry re-creates the fidelity problem they already crowdsource.

D-11 — Overt canaries measure review; catch rate replaces escape rate§

Known-defective statements, one class per row of the research §3 fidelity table (missing hypothesis, junk values, vacuity, quantifier scope, wrong domain, definition mismatch), are seeded into the review queue only — never the claimable frontier — at a disclosed-but-unspecified rate. Reviewers know canaries exist; not which. Caught canaries retire cleanly under a status excluded from D-25's ready set.

Rationale: escape rate counts defects nobody caught and is unmeasurable by construction; covert seeding, once discovered in a public-by-default network, would cost the community trust D-26 exists to earn. Overturns if: crafting non-lazy canaries costs more curator/reviewer time than the measurement is worth — then narrow to the mechanically generatable classes and accept partial coverage.

Detail rules · evidence70 words

Metric change: canary catch rate, per reviewer and per defect class, replaces misformalization escape rate as the measured Stage-1 gate criterion; escape rate is estimated from it, not observed. Per-class catch rates direct mechanical-checker coverage.

Evidence: research §3 — the fidelity-defect taxonomy (missing hypothesis, junk values, vacuity, quantifier scope, wrong domain, definition mismatch) from Ammanamanchi–Bhat–Biderman supplies the canary classes; escape rate is unmeasurable by construction, catch rate is not.

Detail rules · figure · evidence458 words

Offload rule (mechanical). No hole may be definitionally equal to the node's own goal, and a partial whose assembly is a single such hole is rejected at the gate, not reviewed. Restating the goal under a new name is the cheapest available fake progress and the one AlphaProof Nexus observed in practice; it must cost a rejection rather than a hole in the frontier. Partials that are non-trivial but shallow are not policed here — that is what self-selection and the D-25 starvation metric price.

Skeletons are partial proofs (D-31). A Lean skeleton derived from an informal argument — assembly proved, every lemma sorry — is artifact #5 with authored rather than compiler-derived holes; annex, credit and citation rules are in D-31.

Root-level outcomes are research results, with equal status. For an open target, a counterexample at the root (artifact #2) is a full negative resolution of the conjecture — reported upstream per D-10 as a result, not treated as a defect-flavored event. A vacuity certificate at the root (artifact #3) means the conjecture as posed is ill-formed; it is reported to the source registry, not merely routed to internal D-8 revision. A reduction at the root (artifact #4) — "the conjecture holds modulo these named cruxes" — is a primary progress mode when the truth value is unknown, since it converts an open problem into strictly sharper open problems. And a proof of a partial-labeled variant (D-30) is a partial resolution of the target — reported as exactly that.

Evidence: research §1–2 — Goedel-Architect counterexample registration (prove the negation); AlphaProof Nexus caught restated-lemma sorry offloading, motivating artifact #4/#5 discipline.

D-14 — Strategy-level failure records, target-scoped§

D-13 is node-scoped; a dead strategy has no node to attach to. For open problems this decision is central, not supplementary: given the base rate of resolution (research §1 — 9 of 353), refuted routes, dead subgraphs and crux reductions are the expected primary output of most targets, and this record is what makes an unresolved target a contribution rather than a write-off. Three carriers at targets/<target-id>/, descending in trustworthiness:

1. Dead subgraphs are never deleted. A branch killed by a counterexample or abandoned as unreachable is marked abandoned with a cause, and stays queryable with its statements, attempt logs and the artifact that killed it. This is free, fully grounded, and is the primary record.

Rationale: a bad node postmortem wastes one node; a bad strategy verdict can kill a line of attack across a whole target — so the bar for being believed is higher, and grounding, decay and retroactive payment are what raise it. Overturns if: approach records go uncited across a full target lifecycle despite bundle inclusion — then mechanism 3 is a write-only archive and is dropped, keeping 1 and 2.

Detail rules331 words

2. Crux-lemma speculative nodes. A contributor with a stage-1 idea may enter it as a speculative node: the single formal statement the strategy hinges on, body sorry, with a non-vacuity witness. This converts an unfalsifiable idea into a stage-4 object — it typechecks, it can be refuted by a counterexample, it inherits all of D-12 and D-13. An idea that cannot name its crux as a formal statement is not yet recordable.

3. Approach records — free-text, for strategies that never reached a statement. Fields: schema (D-34), route, outcome (same un-collapsible vocabulary as D-13), pinned_mathlib_sha, model_and_tooling, date.

Boundary with D-31. An informal proof of a specific node is not an approach record. It is an annex under D-31, node-scoped, and it converts to work by skeletonization rather than by citation. Mechanism 3 keeps only what D-31 cannot take: strategy-level reasoning with no node and no nameable crux. Where the two could both apply, D-31 governs — its citation is mechanical and mechanism 3's is not.

Credit differs from D-13 and this is the point. Mechanisms 1 and 2 are grounded and earn on merge like any node artifact. Mechanism 3 is not kernel-checkable, is trivially cheap to fabricate, and therefore earns nothing on submission — only retroactively, when a later contributor cites it as having changed their approach. Payment on demonstrated reuse is the only anti-farming rule available when nothing is machine-checkable.

Verdicts decay; node postmortems mostly don't. A strategy declared dead is dead relative to a Mathlib SHA and a generation of tooling. Every approach record carries both, and records predating a pinned-SHA bump or a major capability shift are flagged stale, not authoritative. outcome: blocked on a named library gap auto-resurfaces when the corresponding prerequisite node closes.

Mandatory consumer. Approach records ship in every target-level context bundle (get_target, D-28), so every prover and route-proposer reads them by default. Without a consumer this becomes a write-only archive; here the consumer is mechanical.

D-31 — Informal arguments enter as skeletons; prose is never an artifact§

The empirically dominant open-problem pipeline is informal-first: a general model produces the argument in prose, a Lean system formalizes it. Under D-12 alone that pipeline's load-bearing step has no home — the ledger would pay the transcriber and stiff the prover. This decision gives prose exactly one on-ramp and no other status.

Rationale: skeletonization is the cheapest possible conversion of untrusted prose into kernel-checked structure — a single-session task, far cheaper than proving anything — and it is the highest-leverage contribution type available because it is what expands the frontier; routing prose through it means the network can absorb the informal-first pipeline without ever trusting, storing or paying for an unverifiable claim. Overturns if: skeletons routinely yield holes no harder than the goal despite the offload check, or informal-gap dominates outcomes so heavily that annexes are net context pollution — then skeletonization reverts to D-13 postmortem material only and annexes are dropped.

Skeletonization. The informal argument's lemma structure is written in Lean, every lemma body sorry, and the top-level assembly actually proved:

  have h₁ : A := sorry          -- lemma 1 of the informal argument
  have h₂ : B := sorry          -- lemma 2

A skeleton is D-12 artifact #5 — a partial proof with k holes: it merges to attempts/, never Proof.lean, and the holes enter the frontier as children under D-29 once they pass the statement-side gate.

Rules:

  • Prose attaches as an annex, never as a claim. nodes/<node-id>/annex/<hash>.md — content-hashed, immutable, served as demarcated untrusted data (D-28). An annex earns nothing on submission and nothing later. It is input, not output, and submitting one is not a contribution.
  • The skeleton names the annex it was derived from, by hash. That naming is the mechanical citation D-14 mechanism 3 has never had: reuse here is observable in the artifact rather than self-reported. It is what lets annex authorship be recognized at retrospective write-up (D-19) without being payable at submission.
  • Skeleton holes are authored statements, not compiler-derived. META.yaml carries origin: authored (D-3) — the elaborator did not generate them from a residual goal, a person or agent chose the decomposition. Consequently they are interior nodes, so they carry no fidelity certificate (D-9); and they earn no statement-line credit, which D-19 reserves for roots, defs/, variants and speculative cruxes.
  • The skeletonizer is paid for the assembly. Proof-line credit, flat, for the one thing in the submission the kernel actually checked. Not for the holes, not for the prose, not for k. This is what keeps skeleton volume from being farmable: producing holes is free, and free things earn nothing.
  • Trivial skeletons are rejected mechanically under D-12's offload rule — a single hole definitionally equal to the node's goal is a rename, not a decomposition.
  • A failed skeletonization is a result. If the assembly will not typecheck, the informal argument has a gap or an ambiguity at precisely the joint that will not close. That is a D-13 postmortem with failure_class: informal-gap and a terminal_goal_state localizing it — the mechanical form of what the Erdős #728 forum thread did by hand over two days, and the only cheap check that exists for AI-generated prose.
  • Competing skeletons of one annex are normal. Different decompositions of the same argument produce overlapping holes; that is tolerated frontier noise priced by self-selection (D-25), consolidated only where syntactically duplicate (D-29).
  • Annexes do not gate proving. A node with three annexes confers no obligation to read them. Annex presence is a published frontier field (D-25), so operators can filter toward or away from prose-assisted nodes as they choose.

Evidence: Erdős #728 is the worked example — Sothanaphan's writeup records GPT-5.2 Pro supplying the informal argument, Aristotle formalizing it, and the decisive human step being disambiguation of which statement was meant; Aletheia reports the same shape at scale, with autonomous solutions failing predominantly on statement interpretation and prior art rather than on proof error — neither of which formal verification addresses; AlphaProof Nexus supplies the offload failure mode the trivial-skeleton check exists to catch.

Review, disputes, and revocation§

D-15 — Statement review: separate, adversarial, credited per confirmed defect§

A node's statement reviewer is never its prover and is randomly assigned. Pre-merge reviewer anonymity applies only on bountied nodes (D-26); on standard nodes review is open, like ordinary code review. Credit (ledger by default; payment only on bountied nodes, D-26) attaches to confirmed defects, paired with sample auditing of approvals — the two ship together or not at all. Mechanical checkers run first so human attention goes only to the semantic residue.

Rationale: Aletheia's 68.5% incorrect / 6.5% intended-correct is the empirical case; this is the dominant residual risk in the system. Overturns if: automated fidelity checking becomes good enough to carry interior nodes.

Detail rules · evidence64 words

Open defect claims: anyone — not only the assigned reviewer — may file a statement-defect claim carrying a kernel-checkable exhibit. Confirmed claims earn the defect credit; unconfirmed claims draw down the filer's rejected-claim budget (D-16 — deprioritized, never forfeited).

Evidence: research §2–3 — Aletheia's 68.5%/6.5% figures are the empirical case; LeanMarathon's adversarial Target-Reviewer is the working precedent for separating review from generation.

D-16 — Defect claims are free, shaped, and taxonomy-typed§

No staking. Instead: (1) mechanical pre-triage — a defect claim must name a defect class — missing-hypothesis, junk-value, vacuity, quantifier-scope, wrong-domain, definition-mismatch (D-11's canary classes), strength-drift (D-9's named residual), or other-with-exhibit — and point at a specific line, or it bounces automatically; (2) rolling rejected-claim budget per identity — exhausting it deprioritizes (never blocks) future claims; (3) repeat-pattern rejection feeds the D-22 removal path. Claim classes feed D-11's per-class measurement.

Rationale: the asymmetry is decisive — one escaped fidelity defect poisons a subtree, so the system overpays for false alarms rather than chilling tentative true ones; staking imports a market into the D-26 commons and punishes honest disagreement with noisy panel verdicts. Overturns if: adjudication queue growth is driven by under-the-limit patient flooding — then limits tighten per D-22 standing, still never stakes.

D-17 — Human attention at the root and on defects only§

Automated Target-Review on interior nodes; mandatory human sign-off restricted to target/root statements and defect adjudication.

Detail rules61 words

Disputes have exactly three grounds: (i) the statement doesn't capture the intended mathematics, (ii) the proof exploits a verification loophole, (iii) credit allocation is wrong. (i) and (ii) go to a non-author panel with power to unmerge and revoke. Everything else is not a dispute.

The scaling metric is drain rate on the human sign-off queue, not the PR queue.

D-18 — Revocation is fault-isolated, with immediate quarantine§

When a dispute on grounds (i) or (ii) is accepted for adjudication, the node is flagged disputed and new claims depending on it are blocked immediately. If invalidated: the defective node's credit is revoked; dependents are marked stale but their provers keep ledger credit annotated "correct modulo revoked dependency" — visibly flagged, never silent. The node is repaired via D-8 versioning; dependent proofs are mechanically re-run against the new version and re-annotated on survival.

Rationale: a full cascade punishes innocent downstream work and makes revocation too destructive to invoke; a deterrent the operator hesitates to use is not a deterrent. Overturns if: annotated credit accumulates faster than repairs clear it — then the graph is rotting and the problem is upstream, in the gate or review.

Credit§

D-19 — Credit: an artifact ledger, significance retrospective§

  • Credit is an inclusive ledger (D-26), CRediT-style, not a zero-sum prize.
  • Six lines: statement authorship (roots, defs, variants, speculative cruxes), proof authorship, review, attempts, upstreaming (D-7), write-up (D-32, including explainers under D-3). Model and tool version recorded per node alongside the human name.
  • No prospective weights. The ledger records what was contributed — each entry a kernel-checked or schema-checked artifact. Relative significance is assigned retrospectively at write-up (proposed by the D-32 writer), per target, when realized difficulty is actually known — evidenced by the D-13 record (attempt counts, time-to-close, postmortem volume) and disputable under D-17 ground (iii). Prospective difficulty weights on open problems were guesses without an answer key; the working precedent (ETP's contribution appendix) never had them.
  • Anti-gaming without weights: credit never scales with node count (duplicate and trivial holes are consolidated or ignored at write-up; retrospective significance is the filter splitting can't farm), never with proof length or token spend. Postmortems pay first-per-route-class only (D-13). Contributor-introduced helper lemmas are credit-neutral unless closed as frontier nodes.
  • All credit is versioned and revocable. Retrospective revocation is the real Sybil and collusion defense, not admission control.
  • Identity is the human operator. Agents are tools; every artifact an agent submits is submitted by its operator, who is accountable for it under standing, claim budgets, and the own-credentials rule (D-23). There are no agent identities. Identity creation is costly but not gatekept, and needs no account anywhere: a write token (D-35, POST /tokens) is issued to whoever proves the graph's tutorial node (D-27) through the precheck path — the "one prior merged trivial PR", payable in compute by an agent unattended — under a pseudonym the operator chooses. A GitHub identity is the alternative proof, and either can be attached later to raise rate limits and let credit survive a lost token. Rate-limited per identity. An anonymous identity has no contact channel: every notice about it, including revocation (D-18), is a ledger entry and nothing else. Verification requirements tighten only on evidence of abuse from Stage 0. Operators evading accountability by rotating agents are answered with identity-layer cost, never agent registration.

Revision (D-8): <node-id>@v2 supersedes v1; credit already earned on v1 is kept by its earners per D-18. A revision never re-mints paid credit.

Rationale: the two real contributor populations (enthusiasts on sunk subscription credits, mathematicians needing legible academic credit) need a truthful record and a defensible contribution statement at paper time — the first is artifact-level and mechanical, the second is only assessable after the fact; prospective scalars served neither and were the least-precedented machinery in the design. Overturns if: write-up credit disputes become routine or hard nodes visibly starve for lack of a price signal — then reintroduce per-node scalars, but set retrospectively-informed, never author-fixed.

Metric: ledger entries per identity over time and write-up dispute rate are the monitored series — a dispute spike is the signal that retrospective assignment is failing.

Evidence: research §2, §7 — ETP (repo) author-contribution appendix is the working inclusive-ledger precedent, and it carries no weights; attribution norms per the Leiden Declaration (June 2026, IMU-endorsed); research §7 flags prospective anti-gaming weight rules as unprecedented design reasoning — retired here before Stage 1 rather than tested at contributor expense.

D-22 — Role lifecycle: mechanical floor, human ceiling, published reasoning§

Steady state: track-record thresholds gate eligibility (N merged proofs → reviewer-eligible; M confirmed defects → auditor-eligible); the role is granted by appointment from existing role-holders, Mathlib-maintainer-style; removal requires two role-holders and published reasoning. Rationale: thresholds alone get farmed and cannot verify mathematical judgment; appointment alone is favoritism; credentials verify math but not Lean, so the two competencies seed from different pools. D-32 — Write-up is a role: a staked mathematician converts a target's record into a paper§

Every target that resolves or is declared dormant (D-33) gets a writer: one real-identity mathematician, appointed by the target's curators under D-22, who owns the human-facing account of what the network did. The DAG, ledger and D-13/D-14 record are the network's outputs; the paper is how anyone outside the network consumes them.

Rationale: D-26 promised that the ledger "feeds paper authorship," but no role owned the feeding; a paper is the only network output that referees, hiring committees and the erdosproblems.com forum consume, and a network whose results exist only as kernel-checked artifacts is invisible to the population that supplies its judgment roles. Tying the role to prior stake solves recruitment and conflict of interest with one rule. Overturns if: resolved or dormant targets sit unwritten because no staked contributor will take the role at Stage 3 — then relax the stake requirement first; if still starved, stipend the role, which trips D-24 before any money moves.

Detail rules · evidence434 words
  • Two deliverables, one role. A resolved target (or a proved variant, D-30) gets a paper. A dormant target gets a published state-of-the-problem note: refuted routes, crux reductions, prerequisites opened or upstreamed. At the honest base rate the second is the common case, and it is the deliverable that makes the D-13/D-14 record citable rather than merely stored.
  • Stake required. The writer must already hold statement-line credit (D-19) or curator standing on that target. Writing up a stranger's graph is labor nobody takes; first-authoring the account of a problem one has worked on is the credit mathematicians actually want. This is the recruitment mechanism, not a formality.
  • Exclusion from appointment onward. The writer may not claim proof credit on the target after appointment (credit earned before it stands), and may not hold statement-reviewer or adjudicator role on it — the writer proposes everyone's significance and must not be a party to the disputes that follow. The curator who signed a dormancy declaration may not be appointed writer on that target (D-33).
  • The writer proposes, the ledger decides. Retrospective significance (D-19) and the CRediT-style contribution statement are published with the draft and are disputable under D-17 ground (iii) for a fixed window before submission. Authorship follows the ledger mechanically: ledger entries above a per-target threshold — set by the writer, published with the draft, itself disputable — are coauthors; every other entry is named in the contribution appendix (ETP precedent). No machine authorship; AI involvement disclosed per D-23.

Every theorem claimed cites its node id and gate verdict (D-5); the pinned Lean project is the supplementary material; claims are scoped to the root's fidelity status (D-9). Informal exposition in the paper is D-31 prose: useful, untrusted, and never what the result rests on.

The writer, when appointed, may draw on them and is not bound by them.
  • Credit for writing is the sixth ledger line (D-19) plus the paper's own authorship, which is the externally legible part.

  • The network never holds, routes, or proxies contributor credentials. Pooling or operating accounts on contributors' behalf violates essentially every provider's terms.

  • Data license, fixed here (D-34): the non-code record — attestations, postmortem YAML and goal states, approach records, review and adjudication records — is released under one permissive data license; the sign-off text on every graph states it covers both code and record; annex prose is licensed by its author at submission or not accepted. This is a one-way door: it cannot be relicensed once contributors exist, so it closes with the rest of this decision, before the first external contributor.
  • Not legal advice — counsel before external contributors.

Evidence: research §8 — Mathlib is Apache 2.0; DCO is the Linux precedent; credential pooling is the hard ToS line across providers; Astra's no-machine-authorship/named-critical-readers disclosure is the default, aligned with the Leiden Declaration.

D-24 — Entity: informal now, fiscally sponsored before money or scale§

Stage 1 runs as an informal project under the founder, with two hedges: DCO, not CLA (per-commit sign-off, no receiving entity required, Linux precedent); and a written sunset — fiscal sponsorship (NumFOCUS-class) must be in place before the first bounty is funded or the tenth external contributor joins, whichever first. An owned entity is not built unless a sponsor relationship fails.

Rationale: every trusted precedent (PNT+, ETP, FLT) runs informally; D-26's credit-only default pushes the money trigger out, possibly forever; but CI executing strangers' code and any payment flow are personal-liability cliffs with a cheap, fast off-ramp. Overturns if: a sponsor's policies conflict with pseudonymous contribution or the D-26 ledger — then evaluate C (own entity) against the actual conflict.

D-25 — Distribution is self-selection over a published frontier§

The network publishes the ready set (dependencies closed — the root is always in it once claimable per D-6/D-9) plus speculative nodes and open variants, each advertising dependency and library tags, attempt history, and claim status. Queryable. That is the entire allocation mechanism — no matchmaker, no difficulty router.

Rationale: every human project that works (PNT+, ETP, FLT) uses self-selection over a published list; matchmaking requires estimating difficulty, which nobody can do; self-selection is the only scheme that works identically for a human with an afternoon and an agent with a subscription. Overturns if: hard nodes starve while easy ones oversubscribe on the measured starvation series — first response is bountying starved nodes (D-26), then enriching published fields, never directed allocation.

Detail rules · evidence335 words

Claims carry a TTL declared at claim time within published flat caps — an agent claiming for a two-hour run does not hold a two-week default; undeclared claims get the minimum, not the maximum. Auto-release on expiry, advisory thereafter. Racing is permitted and on hard nodes desirable: first merged fills Proof.lean; a losing racer's complete proof is recorded in attempts/ as an alternate proof and enters the ledger (D-26), or they bank a postmortem.

The frontier publishes observed facts, never difficulty estimates. Self-selection presumes a chooser with taste; an agent has none and will take whatever sorts first. Selection therefore lives in the operator's filter policy — not in the agent, not in the server — and a filter policy is only as expressive as the published fields. Every frontier entry carries: target and relation label; dependency and library tags; origin (authored | compiler-derived | variant | skeleton-hole); attempt count; the route_classes already refuted and the failure_class histogram (D-13); age since entering ready; claim status and claim history; annex presence (D-31); bounty flag. All of these are records of what happened, none is a prediction — which is what keeps this compatible with "no difficulty router" rather than a router by another name. An operator can then write "unattempted skeleton holes on target X with no missing-library failures" and that is self-selection at the level of a predicate.

Starvation is measured, not assumed away. With an agent population, easy nodes oversubscribed and hard nodes untouched is the expected steady state rather than an edge case, so the overturning condition below needs a series it can fire on: the attempt-count distribution across the frontier, and specifically the fraction of ready nodes with zero attempts after N days (pilot default N = 14, recorded per graph). Without it the condition is unfalsifiable.

Evidence: research §2 — PNT+ claim/disclaim protocol, ETP and FLT self-selection over published task lists; races and TTLs address the open-claiming gap research §2 flags as a non-transfer.

D-26 — Public and inclusive by default; exclusivity quarantined to bountied nodes§

The network runs ETP-style: public GitHub PRs, public claiming, public attempt logs. Credit is an inclusive record, not a prize — a CRediT-style public ledger per graph, feeding paper authorship through the D-32 writer. If a node is deliberately bountied (curator decision at intake or on starvation), that node alone uses a private submission queue with hash-chained full disclosure at merge; postmortems always publish on acceptance regardless of channel, and bountied nodes enter the attempt corpus (D-34) only after that disclosure.

Rationale: sniping is only rational when credit is scarce; every community-trusted precedent (ETP, PNT+, FLT) is public-inclusive; the two real contributor populations (enthusiasts on sunk subscription credits, mathematicians needing legible academic credit) are both served by a ledger and neither by a market. Overturns if: hard nodes starve under credit-only incentives at Stage 2 scale — then widen the bountied set, never flip the default.

Prover funnel (agents). Stages§

  • Stage 0 (design review). The protocol itself is posted for review on the Lean Zulip and the erdosproblems.com forum before any open-problem graph is seeded. Two integration agreements are named external dependencies: the erdosproblems.com curator, and the Formal Conjectures maintainers (D-10). An invited run precedes the public pilot: the reference MCP server (D-28) and the git/HTTP path are both live over the tutorial node and one small on-ramp formalization graph (D-6, second-class, ledgered separately — for the invited run it may be a classic textbook result stated over the graph's own defs/ so Mathlib cannot short-circuit it, since the run tests the protocol, not the mathematics), and a handful of invited operators exercise claim → attempt → precheck → submit end-to-end through whichever path they choose; the website (D-36) is live over the same graphs from the same day, so the run also tests whether a bystander can tell what happened. The run is a design-review input, not a pilot: nothing in it counts toward open-problem claims, and its purpose is to find the places where the protocol reads fine and runs badly before the numbering freezes. Two of its measurements are named in advance: precheck latency as operators experience it — the evidence for moving precheck from hosted CI to warm infrastructure — and, for roots still below the step-9 certificate bar, the approval queue — the evidence for whether per-artifact review should survive at all (v3.11 made it conditional on the statement's certificate).
  • Stage 1 (pilot). One seeded graph on a genuinely open target, 20–100 nodes, mixed contributors, manual review; optionally one small on-ramp formalization graph alongside it for protocol calibration, ledgered separately (D-6). Resolving the target is not the success criterion — the honest base rate for open-problem resolution is AlphaProof Nexus's 9/353, not LeanMarathon's 7/7. The pilot succeeds on protocol evidence: closed subgraphs, kernel-checked refuted routes and crux reductions, prerequisite nodes opened/upstreamed, and postmortems demonstrably reused — alongside high canary catch rate across defect classes (D-11) and review latency inside maintainer capacity. The pilot cuts the first D-34 snapshot at close. Treat as a test of D-13, D-15, D-19, D-25, D-29, D-30, D-31, D-33 specifically — reasoned design with no precedent; expect one to need replacing.
  • Stage 2 (scale). Multiple graphs, automated Target-Review, human sign-off on roots only. Change course if the sign-off queue grows faster than it drains.
  • Stage 3 (incentives live). Retrospective write-up credit exercised on a completed target under a D-32 writer, optional bounties. Redesign trigger: duplicate-hole spam, restated-lemma sorry offloading, variant-label gaming, collusive review, or postmortem farming.

Glossary§

Reference: the same definitions that power the hover cards, in one place.

Attempt corpus

the network's complete record of attempts on its targets — gate verdicts pass and fail, postmortems with goal states, strategy records, review and defect records, resolution artifacts — with the model and tooling behind each, released as timestamped snapshots so anyone can train on the resolved past and evaluate on the still-open frontier. A by-product of the ledger, never a purpose of the protocol.

Axiom allowlist

Lean proofs may only rest on three standard axioms; anything else (including the axiom sorry secretly introduces) fails the gate.

Blueprint

the machine-readable record of a target's decomposition: each lemma's formal statement paired with its informal explanation and dependencies. Grown from contributions (partial proofs, reductions, proposed cruxes and variants), not authored up front.

Canary

a deliberately defective statement seeded into the review queue (reviewers know canaries exist, not which) to measure whether review actually works.

CI (continuous integration)

the automated build-and-check infrastructure that runs the gate on every submission.

DCO (Developer Certificate of Origin)

a lightweight per-commit sign-off asserting the contributor has the right to submit the work; the network's licensing mechanism (Linux-kernel precedent), keeping everything Apache-2.0-compatible with Mathlib.

developercertificate.org ↗
Erdős problems

the problems posed by Paul Erdős, catalogued at erdosproblems.com (~600–700 still listed open); the network's primary target class is the genuinely open ones — after a prior-art pass, since "open" empirically often means "solved in scattered literature."

erdosproblems.com ↗
Explainer

a prose account of a merged proof, attached to its node: what the argument does and why it works. Content-hashed and attributed (author and drafting model), but unverified — commentary on a kernel-checked object, never evidence about it. Earns write-up credit; shown on the website under a fixed "unverified" label.

Fidelity certificate

a graded label on a root statement recording how thoroughly its faithfulness has been checked, from mechanical-only up to published-and-uncontested. Claims about a result are scoped to its grade.

Fidelity vs. integrity

the two independent ways "verified" can be false. Integrity: the artifact isn't really a proof of the statement (mechanically catchable). Fidelity: the statement isn't really the mathematics (needs adversarial human review).

Formal Conjectures

DeepMind's public registry of Lean-formalized problem statements; the network inherits statements from it and contributes back rather than maintaining a private registry.

google-deepmind/formal-conjectures ↗
Formalization

translating informal ("paper") mathematics into Lean. The hard part is not proving but stating: a formal statement that subtly differs from the intended one is a misformalization, and the kernel cannot detect it.

Graph / DAG (directed acyclic graph)

the decomposition of a target theorem into lemmas with dependency arrows: lemma A depends on lemmas B and C, which depend on… "Acyclic" means no circular reasoning is possible.

Informal annex

an informal (prose) mathematical argument attached to a node as content-hashed, immutable, untrusted input. It is never a claim and never earns credit; its only route to becoming work is skeletonization.

Kernel replay

re-checking every declaration through the kernel from a clean state, defeating tricks that smuggle unverified declarations into the environment.

Lean 4

a programming language and interactive theorem prover: a system in which mathematical definitions, statements, and proofs are written as code that a small trusted checker (the kernel, ~5,000 lines) verifies mechanically. If the kernel accepts a proof, the proof is correct — full stop.

lean-lang.org ↗
Mathlib

the community-built mathematics library for Lean (~2.3M lines): definitions and theorems from undergraduate through research level that all Lean projects build on. The network pins a specific Mathlib version per target.

github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4 ↗
MCP (Model Context Protocol)

the standard interface AI agents use to call external tools. The network ships an MCP server as a convenience lens; everything it does is equally possible with plain git and HTTP, by design.

modelcontextprotocol.io ↗
native_decide

a Lean tactic that trusts the compiler instead of the kernel to check a computation. Banned by default here because it massively widens what must be trusted; waivers are recorded and flagged.

Node

one lemma in the graph, packaged as a directory: its immutable statement, a witness, its dependencies' signatures, a slot for the proof, and a log of attempts. The node is the unit of claiming, submission, and credit.

Olean cache

precompiled Lean build artifacts. With a warm cache a build takes minutes; cold, hours. Required infrastructure for agent onboarding and CI economy.

Postmortem

the structured, typed record of a failed proof attempt: route tried, outcome, exact terminal proof state. Not a transcript.

Root / interior node

the root is the target theorem itself (the thing the outside world cares about); interior nodes are the supporting lemmas. Fidelity review concentrates on roots and definitions because an interior lemma's meaning doesn't matter — only its formal role does.

Skeleton / skeletonization

the Lean rendering of an informal argument's lemma structure: every lemma stated with body sorry, and the top-level assembly actually proved. If it typechecks, the argument's architecture is kernel-checked even though none of its content is, and the open lemmas become claimable nodes.

sorry

Lean's placeholder for a missing proof. A file containing sorry compiles but proves nothing. "Sorry-free" = actually proved.

Statement vs. proof

the statement is what is claimed (the theorem's exact formal wording); the proof is why it holds. In this network statements are immutable and reviewed; proofs are what contributors supply.

The gate

the automated checklist every submission must pass: pinned toolchain, untouched statement, sandboxed build, kernel replay from a clean environment, axiom allowlist, hazard checkers, witness present, dependencies declared, non-author review. Fail-closed: any miss rejects.

Trust base

the components a verdict's correctness depends on: the graph repository's history and the gate pinned to run on it. Everything else — the MCP server, the HTTP service, the website — is a convenience over the trust base and can be compromised or lost without any verdict changing.

Upstreaming

contributing generally useful lemmas back to Mathlib. Results stay in the network; infrastructure goes to the commons.

Variant

an immutable alternative statement of a root, labeled by its kernel-checked relation to it (resolves, partial, or related); proving a partial variant is a partial resolution of the target.

Witness (non-vacuity witness)

a concrete example satisfying a statement's hypotheses. Without one, a statement with contradictory hypotheses is "provable" trivially and worthlessly; requiring a witness closes that exploit.
