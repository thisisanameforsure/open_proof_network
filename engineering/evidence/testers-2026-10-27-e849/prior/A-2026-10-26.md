## Notes on the open core, t ≥ 5 (log only, nothing on the record; citations from memory, B should verify)

Translation. Row count t(a) vs Singmaster's N(a) (all positions 0<k<n): every row n with a = C(n,k), k < n/2 contributes 2 positions, the central k = n/2 contributes 1. So t(a) ≥ 5 ⇔ a has ≥ 5 rows, i.e. N(a) ≥ 9 (or 10 with no central). Row a itself (k=1) is always one of them, so t ≥ 5 needs FOUR rows with 2 ≤ k ≤ n/2, i.e. three simultaneous nontrivial collisions C(n1,k1) = C(n2,k2) = C(n3,k3) = C(n4,k4) with all ki ≥ 2. Known record: 3003 has 4 rows (N = 8), the only known a with N = 8, none known with N ≥ 9.

What it would take for t ≥ 5. Four distinct pairs (n_i,k_i), 2 ≤ k_i ≤ n_i/2, with equal values. Distinct rows force distinct k_i (larger n ⇒ smaller k for equal value, since C(·,k) increases in n and in k up to n/2), so the k_i are four distinct values ≥ 2; the smallest k is ≥2, so the largest n is ~ sqrt(2a) and the others are smaller. Any such a gives a solution of every pairwise equation C(x,k_i) = C(y,k_j) for the six pairs (k_i,k_j). So:
- every pair (k,l) for which C(x,k)=C(y,l) is completely solved rules out t ≥ 5 configurations using that pair, unless a is one of its finitely many listed values (which can be checked to have t ≤ 4).

Solved collision equations (as I recall them — verify):
- C(n,k) = C(n-1,k+1) (adjacent rows): solved completely, the Fibonacci family n = F_{2i+2}F_{2i+3}, k = F_{2i}F_{2i+3} (Lind 1968; Singmaster 1975, Fibonacci Quart.). Gives infinitely many a with N ≥ 6 (t ≥ 3). Its values beyond 3003 are not known to have a fourth row.
- C(n,2) = C(m,3): Avanesov (1966): nontrivial m = 10, 22, 36 (values 120, 1540, 7140).
- C(n,2) = C(m,4): Pintér (1995) / de Weger (1996): only 210 = C(21,2) = C(10,4).
- C(n,3) = C(m,4): de Weger (1996): only trivial? (to check).
- C(n,2) = C(m,5), C(n,2) = C(m,6), C(n,2) = C(m,8)? Bugeaud, Mignotte, Siksek, Stoll, Tengely (2008, Compositio, "Integral points on hyperelliptic curves") solved C(n,5) = C(m,2): solutions include 3003 = C(15,5) = C(78,2) and 10 = C(5,2)... (list to check).
- C(n,3)=C(m,5)? C(n,4)=C(m,6)? I believe several small pairs were done by Stroeker–de Weger (elliptic logarithms) for C(n,k)=C(m,l) with small (k,l); exact list to verify (Blokhuis, Brouwer, de Weger, "Binomial collisions and near collisions", Integers 17 (2017) A64, summarises and extends: all collisions with values < 10^60 (?) are the known ones).
So any a with t ≥ 5 must use four distinct k's, most combinations of which involve some k ≥ 4 pair not yet solved; and a must exceed the search bound (~10^30–10^60 per BBdW, verify).

Upper bounds (refutation side): N(a) = O(log a) (Singmaster 1971, Amer. Math. Monthly 78); O(log a / log log a) (Abbott, Erdős, Hanson 1974, Amer. Math. Monthly 81); O(log a · logloglog a / (loglog a)^3) (Kane 2007). Matomäki, Radziwiłł, Shao, Tao, Teräväinen (2021/22, "Singmaster's conjecture in the interior of Pascal's triangle", Q. J. Math.): for k in the range exp((log n)^{2/3+ε}) ≤ k ≤ n/2 there are at most two solutions (one row pair, the Fibonacci kind), for a large.

Where it gets stuck.
- Refuting the root = proving t(a) ≤ T for some absolute T (essentially Singmaster's conjecture, row form). Interior range is handled (MRSTT); the obstruction is small and medium k: 2 ≤ k ≤ exp((log n)^{2/3}). For each fixed pair (k,l) the curve C(x,k) = C(y,l) has finitely many integer points (Siegel/Faltings for genus ≥ 1, with effective Baker bounds only for some, e.g. hyperelliptic l=2), but there is no uniform treatment over all pairs, and the number of pairs grows with a. So: finiteness per pair, no uniformity.
- Proving the root needs, for t = 5, one explicit a (none below the search bound) or an infinite family with ≥ 5 rows; the only infinite family known (Fibonacci) is a one-parameter Pell-type family for ONE collision; t ≥ 5 needs three independent collisions at once, i.e. integer points on an intersection of three curves — heuristically finitely many (expected zero), which is why most believe the answer is no.
- Lean-wise: none of the solved collision results are formalised; the most formalisable is Avanesov's C(n,2)=C(m,3) (elliptic curve integral points — heavy) — the Fibonacci adjacent-row case is elementary (Pell/Cassini) and its converse (all solutions are Fibonacci) is the elementary formalisable target: it would show "two adjacent rows ⇒ Fibonacci", a real structural step. Also elementary: k_i distinct across rows (equal value, equal k ⇒ equal n).
