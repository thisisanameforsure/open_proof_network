Prior art and the state of the problem (Erdős #1094, the Erdős–Lacampagne–Selfridge conjecture), with a reading of the formal statement against it.

**1. The statement, read against the Lean.** The root says the set of pairs (n, k) with 0 < k, 2k ≤ n and minFac(C(n,k)) > max(⌊n/k⌋, k) is finite. The floor is harmless: minFac is an integer, and for an integer p, p > ⌊n/k⌋ holds exactly when p > n/k, so the Lean set is the set of exceptions to "the least prime factor of C(n,k) is ≤ max(n/k, k)" with real division, as erdosproblems.com/1094 states it. k = 1 contributes nothing (C(n,1) = n and minFac n ≤ n). The hypothesis n ≥ 2k is needed: without it C(n, n−1) = n gives infinitely many exceptions (n prime). C(n,k) ≥ 2 throughout, so Mathlib's convention minFac 1 = 1 never arises. I found no fidelity defect. The Lean is the "finitely many exceptions" form; the 1988 paper conjectures more precisely that the exceptions are exactly the fourteen listed below, which the Lean does not say (it is weaker, as erdosproblems states it).

**2. Where max(n/k, k) switches.** For 2k ≤ n < k², ⌊n/k⌋ < k, so the bound is k and an exception is exactly a *good* binomial coefficient in the sense of Ecklund–Erdős–Selfridge: every prime factor of C(n,k) exceeds k. For n ≥ k² the bound is n/k. The two regimes have very different status.

**3. The regime 2k ≤ n < k² is settled for large k (by deep analytic results, not formalised).** Let g(k) be the least n > k + 1 with C(n,k) good (erdosproblems #1095). Ecklund, Erdős and Selfridge (1974) proved k^(1+c) < g(k) ≤ exp((1+o(1))k); Erdős, Lacampagne and Selfridge (1993) proved g(k) > ck²/log k; Granville and Ramaré (1996) proved g(k) ≥ exp(c (log³ k / log log k)^(1/2)); Konyagin (1999) proved g(k) ≫ exp(c (log k)²). Either of the last two exceeds k² for all large k, so for k beyond some K₀ there is no exception with n < k², and for each of the finitely many smaller k there are finitely many n < k². Hence the exceptions with n < k² form a finite set: this half of the conjecture is a theorem. I have not checked whether the constants are explicit, so K₀ may be ineffective. Sorenson, Sorenson and Webster (2020) computed g(k) for k ≤ 375 (g(375) ≈ 1.29·10³¹), which is far above k²; note that g(k) is only the *least* good n, so it rules out exceptions with n < k² only when g(k) ≥ k².

**4. The regime n ≥ k² is the open part.** Here the conjecture says C(n,k) has a prime factor ≤ n/k with finitely many exceptions. This is the finitely-many form of Ecklund's conjecture (Ecklund 1969: whenever n > k², C(n,k) has a prime factor p < n/k); Selfridge (1977) conjectured it for all n ≥ k² − 1 with the single exception C(62,6), whose least prime factor is 19 > 62/6. Only weaker bounds of the form p ≪ n/k^c are known (erdosproblems #384). Guy (B31, B33) credits Selfridge with the conjecture that n > 17.125k suffices. A consistency check: since section 3 is a theorem, a proof of this regime would resolve #1094, which is listed as open. What is elementary here is the fixed-k statement (Erdős's observation): for each k the exceptions with n ≥ k² satisfy n < k! + k. Proof: m = k·⌊n/k⌋ has all prime factors ≤ n/k (those of k are ≤ k ≤ n/k when n ≥ k²), so if C(n,k) has none, m is coprime to C(n,k); m is one of n−k+1, …, n, so it divides n(n−1)…(n−k+1) = k!·C(n,k), hence m divides k!, and n − k < m ≤ k!. The difficulty is uniformity in k.

**5. What was proved in 1969, exactly.** Ecklund, "On prime divisors of the binomial coefficient", Pacific J. Math. 29 (1969) 267–270: if n ≥ 2k then C(n,k) has a prime divisor p ≤ max(n/k, n/2) (that is, p ≤ n/2 for k ≥ 2), with the single exception C(7,3) = 5·7. This answered a conjecture of Erdős and Selfridge (erdosproblems #384). It does not give the bound n/k.

**6. The known exceptions and the computations.** Erdős, Lacampagne and Selfridge (1988) list fourteen exceptions (n, k) with least prime factor in brackets: (7,3)[5], (13,4)[5], (14,4)[7], (23,5)[7], (62,6)[19], (44,8)[11], (46,10)[11], (47,10)[11], (74,10)[11], (94,10)[11], (95,10)[11], (47,11)[13], (241,16)[17], (284,28)[29]. Only (62,6) has n ≥ k². In 1993 (as summarised in an answer to MathOverflow question 368183) they report a search for C(n,k) with least prime factor p > max(n/k, 5) for k ≤ 12000 and say it is possible that the bound max(n/k, 13) holds with only twelve exceptions; their abstract states the conjecture p ≤ max(N/k, 29). They also conjecture that their list of 17 good coefficients of deficiency > 1 is complete. I re-checked the bound mechanically for every k ≤ 200 and 2k ≤ n ≤ 200000 (Kummer's carry criterion, one carry in base p of k + (n − k) iff p divides C(n,k)): the exceptions in that box are exactly the fourteen above. This is an independent check of a finite box, not of the literature's full range.

**7. Consequences for decomposition.** A split at n = k² separates a settled-but-deep half (n < k², which would need Konyagin or Granville–Ramaré formalised) from the open half (n ≥ k², Ecklund's conjecture). Within n ≥ k², the fixed-k bound of section 4 is elementary and formalisable; the remaining content is a bound on k alone for the exceptions with n ≥ k².
