Status of this statement: it is a theorem in the literature (for all large k, no good C(n,k) has n < k²), by analytic methods nobody has formalised; a route and what an elementary attack gives.

**1. What it says.** There is K such that if 2k ≤ n < k² and every prime factor of C(n,k) exceeds k (C(n,k) is *good*), then k ≤ K. Write g(k) for the least n > k + 1 with C(n,k) good (erdosproblems #1095). The statement follows from g(k) > k² for all large k, since then no good C(n,k) has n < k².

**2. The literature proves g(k) > k² for large k.** Ecklund, Erdős and Selfridge, Math. Comp. 28 (1974) 647–649, proved k^(1+c) < g(k) ≤ exp((1 + o(1))k). Erdős, Lacampagne and Selfridge, Math. Comp. 61 (1993) 215–224, proved g(k) > ck²/log k, which just misses k². Granville and Ramaré, Mathematika 43 (1996) 73–107, proved g(k) ≥ exp(c (log³ k / log log k)^(1/2)) using explicit bounds for exponential sums; Konyagin, Mathematika 46 (1999) 41–55, improved this to g(k) ≫ exp(c (log k)²) with a new theorem on the distribution of fractional parts of smooth functions. Either of the last two exceeds k² once k is large. I have not checked whether their constants are explicit; if they are not, K is ineffective, which does not matter for an existence statement.

**3. Why the problem is a fractional-parts problem.** By Kummer, a prime p ≤ k fails to divide C(n,k) exactly when adding k and n − k in base p has no carry, that is, when every base-p digit of k is at most the corresponding digit of n. For a prime p in (√k, k] there are two digits, and the condition becomes a statement about the fractional part {n/p} compared with {k/p}: roughly, n mod p must be at least k mod p. Goodness of C(n,k) asks this simultaneously for every prime p ≤ k. For n < k², n/p ranges over values below k²/p, and (this is my summary of the approach, not a quotation from the papers) the analytic bounds show that the points ({n/p}) for the primes p in a range like (k/2, k] cannot all land on the required side, by equidistribution of n/p modulo 1 as p varies (exponential sums over primes, or Konyagin's fractional-parts theorem). An elementary count gives only the ELS-type bound ck²/log k.

**4. Computation.** Every exception to the root with 2k ≤ n < k² is a good C(n,k) with n < k². In the box k ≤ 200, 2k ≤ n ≤ 200000 the only ones are the thirteen in the 1988 list other than C(62,6): (7,3), (13,4), (14,4), (23,5), (44,8), (46,10), (47,10), (74,10), (94,10), (95,10), (47,11), (241,16), (284,28). The largest k among them is 28. Sorenson, Sorenson and Webster (ANTS XIV, Open Book Series 4 (2020) 371–385) computed g(k) for k ≤ 375; g(k) is only the least good n, so these values settle a given k only when g(k) ≥ k².

**5. For a decomposition.** A formal proof would need either an explicit Granville–Ramaré-type bound or Konyagin's theorem; I know of no elementary proof of g(k) > k². A smaller, possibly formalisable target is the ELS bound g(k) > ck²/log k, which does not suffice on its own.
