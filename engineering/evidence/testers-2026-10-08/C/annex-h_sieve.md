The residue form of the small-n regime: what is known, what is not, and the numbers.

**1. What it says, and why it implies its parent.** For all large k and every n with 2k ≤ n < k², some prime p ≤ k has n mod p < k mod p. By Kummer's theorem such a p divides C(n,k): adding k and n − k in base p carries out of the last digit exactly when (n − k) mod p + k mod p ≥ p, that is when n mod p < k mod p. So the statement implies that C(n,k) has a prime factor ≤ k, which is the parent hole's conclusion. It is stronger than the parent: it uses only the last base-p digit, while C(n,k) can have the factor p through a carry in a higher digit.

**2. Status in the literature (not verified against the papers).** Write g(k) for the least n > k + 1 with C(n,k) free of primes ≤ k. Granville and Ramaré (Mathematika 43 (1996) 73–107) and Konyagin (Mathematika 46 (1999) 41–55) prove g(k) > k² for all large k (in fact g(k) ≥ exp(c (log³ k / log log k)^(1/2)), resp. g(k) ≫ exp(c (log k)²)). Konyagin's abstract advertises a theorem on the distribution of fractional parts of a smooth function, and n mod p < k mod p is the same as {n/p} < {k/p}, so the last-digit form is the natural one for these methods. I could not read either paper (paywalled), so I cannot confirm that they prove exactly this last-digit statement over the whole range 2k ≤ n < k², rather than a statement using other digits or a restricted set of primes. Treat "this hole is a known theorem" as plausible, not established.

**3. Heuristic.** For a prime p in (k/2, k], k mod p = k − p, and the chance that n mod p ≥ k − p is 1 − (k − p)/p. Multiplying over those primes gives about exp(−c k / log k), so among the fewer than k² values of n the expected number of failures tends to 0 very fast. This matches the heuristic log g(k) ≍ k / log k (Sorenson, Sorenson and Webster, ANTS XIV, Open Book Series 4 (2020) 371–385).

**4. Computation.** I checked every k ≤ 1000 and every 2k ≤ n < k² directly (about 3·10⁸ pairs): there are 301 pairs with no prime p ≤ k satisfying n mod p < k mod p, the largest k among them is 58, and the last is (n, k) = (1579, 58). So in this range the statement holds with K = 58, and nothing new appears for 58 < k ≤ 1000. Note that K here is larger than for the parent (the largest k with a good C(n,k), n < k², in the 1988 list is 28), because the last-digit condition is stronger than divisibility.
