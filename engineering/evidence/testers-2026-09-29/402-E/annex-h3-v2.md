Hole erdos-402--h3-v2: what it asks, the two parts of it that are elementary, and the part that is not.

What it asks. Its first two hypotheses are the statements of the proved holes h1 and h2-v2; they are true and play no part. What remains: for every finite set A of positive integers with gcd(A) = 1, n = |A|, such that every ratio a/b (a, b in A) in lowest terms u/v has u, v <= n, some pair has a/gcd(a, b) >= n. If the conclusion failed, every a/gcd(a, b) would be at most n - 1, so the ratio hypothesis holds automatically; hence h3-v2 is exactly Graham's gcd conjecture (Erdős 402) for sets with gcd 1, and with h2-v2 it is the whole conjecture. The implication root -> h3-v2 is a two-line specialisation (intro, apply the root), checked on POST /check (okay true). So h3-v2 is (a) in the sense that it carries the full Balasubramanian-Soundararajan theorem; it is not reduced by the parent skeleton.

Numerics (Python, exhaustive, not machine-checked in Lean). Over all n-subsets of {1..N} for (n, N) = (2, 60), (3, 60), (4, 48), (5, 40), (6, 36), (7, 30), (8, 26), and over all n-subsets of the divisors of lcm(1..n) for n <= 8, the sets meeting h3-v2's hypotheses are exactly Graham's equality cases {1, ..., n}, {L/n, ..., L/1} with L = lcm(1..n), and for n = 4 also {2, 3, 4, 6} and {3, 4, 6, 12}; every one of them has max a/gcd(a, b) = n exactly. No failure of the conclusion. So the hypothesis class of h3-v2 is tiny, and a proof of h3-v2 is really a proof that no primitive set has all a/gcd(a, b) <= n - 1.

Two elementary parts (both proved inside the skeleton that cites this annex; the whole file was fast-checked with POST /check, okay true, only the hole's sorry remaining). Assume gcd(A) = 1 and, for contradiction, a < n * gcd(a, b) for all a, b in A.

1. Prime powers. If p is prime and p^k divides some a in A, then p^k < n. Since gcd(A) = 1 there is b in A with p not dividing b; then p^k is coprime to g = gcd(a, b), so p^k divides a/g, which is below n. In particular every element of A divides lcm(1, ..., n - 1).

2. Prime size. If n = p is prime, the conclusion holds. By 1 with k = 1, p divides no element, so a -> a mod p sends the p elements of A into the p - 1 non-zero residues, and two distinct elements a, b collide. Write g = gcd(a, b), a = g x, b = g y. Then x, y < p by the assumption, and p does not divide g (g divides a), so g x = g y (mod p) gives x = y (mod p), hence x = y and a = b, a contradiction. This uses neither the ratio hypothesis of h3-v2 nor anything beyond pigeonhole; it is the case |A| prime of Graham's conjecture, all primes at once (the proved variants cover only |A| <= 8 and sets containing a large prime or an element with large least prime factor).

What remains (the skeleton's hole). Composite n: a primitive set A of composite size n with a <= n * gcd(a, b) for all a, b and every prime power dividing an element below n satisfies the bound. Its hypotheses are satisfiable ({1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}), so the hole can be witnessed. For n a prime power q = p^e (e >= 2) the hypothesis still says q divides no element, but the residue argument no longer closes: p may divide gcd(a, b), so g x = g y (mod q) does not give x = y (mod q). This hole is still the conjecture for composite n and should not be expected to be elementary: Szegedy (1986) and Zaharescu (1987) proved Graham's conjecture for all sufficiently large n, and R. Balasubramanian and K. Soundararajan, "On a conjecture of R. L. Graham", Acta Arithmetica 75 (1996), 1-38, proved it for every n. A prover should formalise their argument for composite n, or split the hole further along its cases (the finite check of annex 48352dbc and e3b2ad47 on the root handles each fixed n up to at least 10).
